# Course 5: Public Speaking and Storytelling

**For learners 13–19 · Self-paced · ~8 hours**

The person who can stand in front of a room and hold attention has an unfair advantage. Always has. Always will. In an age where everyone's faces are in their phones, the people who can speak well in real life will run things.

This isn't just about giving school presentations without dying inside. It's about being able to pitch an idea, lead a meeting, tell a story at dinner that everyone remembers, give a wedding toast, motivate a team, change minds. Real life skills.

## Module 1: Why Speaking Matters

### Lesson 1.1 — The compounding advantage

People who can speak well in public get more opportunities than people who can't. Period. Not because they're smarter. Because:

- Teachers notice them
- Interview panels remember them
- Audiences trust them
- Investors back them
- Followers gather around them
- Decisions get made in rooms where speaking happens

The skill compounds. Every time you speak well, more people see you. More people seeing you means more chances to speak. Which means more practice. Which means you get better. The flywheel spins for life.

### Lesson 1.2 — Why most people are terrible at it

Watch any school assembly, wedding speech, or work presentation. Most people are bad. They speak in monotone, ramble, hide behind slides, never look up. Not because they can't be better — because nobody taught them, and they think being smart means sounding formal.

The bar for excellent public speaking is shockingly low. Five hours of practice will put you ahead of most adults you'll ever encounter.

### Lesson 1.3 — Speaking vs presenting vs storytelling

These are different but related:

- **Speaking** — broad term for talking to people
- **Presenting** — speaking in a structured way to communicate information or persuade
- **Storytelling** — using narrative (with characters, conflict, resolution) to move people

You'll learn all three. Storytelling is the highest leverage because every other form of speaking benefits when you tell more stories.

### Exercise 1.1 — Speaker audit

Watch 3 TED talks. For each, rate from 1–10: How interesting was the speaker physically? How clear was the structure? How memorable were the stories? How much would you trust this person? Note what they did differently from each other.

### Exercise 1.2 — Your speaking history

Write down every time you've spoken in public (at school, family events, anywhere). Rate each from 1–10. Note: what went well? What went badly? What do you wish you'd known?

## Module 2: Handling Fear and Nerves

### Lesson 2.1 — The truth about nerves

You will never stop feeling nervous before speaking. Professional TED speakers feel nervous. Politicians feel nervous. Comedians who've performed 10,000 shows feel nervous.

The goal isn't to eliminate nerves. The goal is to perform well *with* nerves. Big difference.

### Lesson 2.2 — Where the fear comes from

Public speaking ranks among the most common fears people have — sometimes ranked higher than fear of death. Why?

Evolution. For most of human history, being judged badly by your tribe could mean exile, which meant death. Your brain still treats a hostile audience like an existential threat. Knowing this doesn't eliminate the fear, but it helps you reframe it: this is an old instinct, not a real danger.

### Lesson 2.3 — Physical techniques that actually work

**Box breathing.** Before going on: breathe in for 4 seconds, hold for 4, exhale for 4, hold for 4. Do this 4–6 times. It calms your nervous system fast.

**Power posing.** Stand tall, shoulders back, hands on hips (the "Wonder Woman pose"), for 2 minutes before speaking. The research on this is debated, but plenty of people swear by it.

**Movement.** Walk around backstage before going on. Shake out your arms. Stretch. Don't sit hunched over.

**Warm up your voice.** Hum. Say tongue twisters. Read something out loud. A warm voice carries better than a cold one.

### Lesson 2.4 — Mental techniques that actually work

**Reframe the nerves.** Instead of "I'm scared," say "I'm excited." Same physical sensation (elevated heart rate, alert mind). Different label. Your brain will go with it.

**Focus on the audience, not yourself.** Most fear is self-focused: "What will they think of me?" Replace it with: "What do they need from me?" Service-mode beats performance-mode every time.

**Visualize success.** Spend 2 minutes before speaking imagining it going well. Yes, this works. Athletes use it. Speakers use it. Free.

**Have a pre-show ritual.** Before every talk, do the same 5 things. Brain associates ritual with calm. Could be: bathroom, water, breathe, look at notes, walk to spot.

### Lesson 2.5 — What to do if you freeze on stage

It happens. Plan for it:

- **Pause.** A 5-second silence feels eternal to you. To the audience, it's barely noticed. Use it.
- **Drink water.** Always have water on stage. Drinking buys you 10 seconds to recover.
- **Be honest.** "Give me a second, I lost my thread." Audiences forgive humans. They're harder on robots.
- **Have a fallback line.** Something like "Let me come back to that — first let me tell you about..." Move on.

### Exercise 2.1 — The 2-minute speech

This week, give a 2-minute speech in front of at least 3 people. Topic: "Something I learned recently." Use box breathing beforehand. Notice what nerves feel like in your body.

### Exercise 2.2 — Record yourself

Record yourself giving any 2-minute speech. Watch it back. Notice nervous habits (saying "um," shifting weight, looking down). These are your data. You can't fix what you don't see.

### Exercise 2.3 — Build your pre-show ritual

Design a 5-step routine you'll do before any speaking event. Write it down. Test it. Adjust it. Use it every time.

## Module 3: Voice, Body, Presence

### Lesson 3.1 — Voice basics

Most speakers underuse their voice. They speak in a flat line. Boring. To use your voice well:

**Volume.** Loud enough that the back row hears clearly. Most beginners are too quiet. When in doubt, project more.

**Pace.** Vary it. Important points slow down. Stories speed up. Pause before key lines. Silence is a tool.

**Pitch.** Don't speak in monotone. Voice should go up and down naturally. To find your range: read a kids' book out loud, exaggerated. That's your range. Use 60% of it.

**Tone.** Match the content. Sad stories need a softer voice. Funny moments need lightness. Serious points need weight.

### Lesson 3.2 — The pause is your superpower

Beginners fear silence. They fill every gap with "um," "like," "you know."

Confident speakers use silence deliberately. After a key point: pause. Before answering a hard question: pause. To regain attention: pause.

A 2-second pause can land an idea more than 20 words can.

### Lesson 3.3 — Body language

Your body talks before your mouth does. Audiences read your posture before your first word.

**Stance.** Feet shoulder-width apart. Weight even on both feet. Don't sway, don't rock. Plant.

**Hands.** Use them to emphasize, never to fidget. If you don't know what to do with them, let them rest at your sides between gestures. (Yes, this feels awkward. It looks normal.)

**Eye contact.** Connect with one person at a time, hold for 3–5 seconds, move to another person. Don't scan the room like a security camera. Don't stare at one person the whole talk.

**Movement.** Walk with purpose, not anxiety. Standing still is fine. Walking randomly is not. If you move, move because you're transitioning to a new section or making a point.

### Lesson 3.4 — Presence

Presence is hard to define but everyone knows it when they see it. Some elements:

- **Slow down.** Nervous speakers rush. Confident speakers take their time.
- **Take up space.** Don't shrink into the corner of the stage. Own it.
- **Hold attention without demanding it.** Audiences sense desperation. They also sense calm authority.
- **Speak like the message matters.** Because it does.

Presence isn't a personality trait. It's a set of behaviors. You can build it.

### Lesson 3.5 — Filler words

"Um, like, so, you know, basically." Filler words leak credibility. They suggest your brain hasn't caught up to your mouth.

How to reduce them:
1. **Notice them first.** Record yourself, count them.
2. **Replace fillers with pauses.** When your brain reaches for "um," pause instead. Silence is more confident.
3. **Slow down.** Most fillers come from rushing.

You'll never eliminate filler completely. Cutting them in half is a huge upgrade.

### Exercise 3.1 — The voice range drill

Read a paragraph from any book using these voices:
1. Whispered, nervous
2. Loud, angry
3. Slow, dramatic
4. Fast, excited
5. Normal but with deliberate pauses

Notice how much range you have. You'll only use a fraction in real speaking, but knowing the full range gives you tools.

### Exercise 3.2 — The body scan

Stand in front of a mirror. Give a 2-minute speech on anything. Watch your body. Identify:
- 3 things your body does well
- 3 nervous habits
- 1 thing to start doing more

### Exercise 3.3 — The filler word count

Record a 5-minute conversation with a friend (with permission). Listen back and count your filler words. Don't judge — just count. Notice when they spike (when you're explaining something complex, when you're not sure, etc.).

## Module 4: Storytelling — The Cheat Code

### Lesson 4.1 — Why stories beat facts

Humans evolved listening to stories around fires before they invented writing. Our brains are wired for narrative. We forget statistics. We remember stories.

If you tell me unemployment is 33%, I'll forget by tomorrow. If you tell me about Chika, a 24-year-old computer science graduate in Lagos who's been job-hunting for 18 months and just took a job selling phone chargers at a market — I'll remember that for years.

The fact is the same. The story makes it stick.

### Lesson 4.2 — The story structure (again)

Every story has roughly the same shape:

1. **A character** we care about
2. **In a specific situation**
3. **Wants something**
4. **Faces an obstacle**
5. **Tries to overcome it**
6. **Succeeds or fails**
7. **Is changed by what happened**

When you tell a story, make sure all seven elements are there. Missing any of them = the story falls flat.

### Lesson 4.3 — Specifics make stories alive

Bad story: "I had a difficult experience that taught me resilience."

Better story: "When I was 14, I tried to start a YouTube channel about poetry. I posted three videos. Each one got less than 30 views. Most of those were my mother. After three months I deleted the channel and cried for two days. But six months later I started writing poetry just for me — not for views — and that's the year I actually got good. That YouTube failure was the best thing that happened to my writing."

Specifics. Names, ages, numbers, settings. They give the story texture and make it real.

### Lesson 4.4 — Vulnerability without oversharing

The best stories include moments where you looked bad — failed, was scared, was confused, was wrong. Vulnerability is the door audiences walk through to connect with you.

But vulnerability has limits. Don't dump trauma on people who didn't ask. Don't reveal stuff that hurts other people. Don't share to seek pity. Share to connect, illustrate, or teach.

Test: would this story make a stranger see something they didn't see before? If yes, share. If it's just for catharsis, write it in your journal instead.

### Lesson 4.5 — The one-minute story

A great compact story has:
- A clear character (often you)
- A specific setting (date, place, situation)
- A turning point
- A landing line (the meaning)

Practice telling one-minute stories. They're the building blocks of every speech you'll ever give.

**Example one-minute story:**

> When I was 13, my dad gave me ₦5,000 and told me to come back from the market with food for the whole family. I'd never shopped for the family before. I came back with the wrong meat, the wrong rice, and somehow spent the whole budget on three items.
>
> My mom didn't yell. She just sat me down and walked me through how to budget for a meal. The next time, I came back with proper shopping for everyone.
>
> That ₦5,000 lesson was the first time I understood what budgeting actually was. Years later when I started my first side hustle, I caught myself remembering that day every time I priced something.

One minute. Setup, conflict, turn, meaning. That structure works for almost any point you want to make.

### Lesson 4.6 — Stories you can tell

Build a personal library of 10–15 stories from your own life. Categories that always work:

- A time you failed at something and what you learned
- A time someone helped you when they didn't have to
- A time you helped someone and it changed how you saw yourself
- A time you were wrong about something important
- A time you took a risk and it worked
- A time you took a risk and it didn't
- A formative moment with a family member
- A time you stood up for something
- A time you should have stood up but didn't
- A moment that changed how you see the world

These are your stories. Practice telling them. Refine them. You'll use them in essays, interviews, speeches, conversations for the rest of your life.

### Exercise 4.1 — Build your story library

Write down 10 personal stories from your life. For each, one paragraph. Don't worry about polish yet — just capture the raw material.

### Exercise 4.2 — Tell a story to a friend

Pick one of your stories. Tell it out loud to a friend. Watch their face. Where did they lean in? Where did they zone out? Adjust the story based on what worked.

### Exercise 4.3 — The TED-style story

Watch any TED talk that goes viral. Identify the first story the speaker tells. Map it to the story structure (character, situation, want, obstacle, attempt, result, change). You'll see the pattern.

## Module 5: Structuring Talks That Land

### Lesson 5.1 — The opening (60 seconds that determine everything)

Audiences decide in the first minute whether they're going to actually listen. Don't waste it.

**Bad openings:**
- "Hi, my name is..." (they read the program)
- "I'm so honored to be here..." (so what?)
- "Today I'm going to talk about..." (just talk about it)
- Apologizing for being nervous

**Good openings:**
- A short, intriguing story
- A surprising statistic with a specific human behind it
- A bold claim that challenges what people believe
- A question that makes the audience think
- A scene that drops them into the action

### Lesson 5.2 — The single-message rule

Every talk should have ONE main message. One. Not three. Not seven.

If your audience could only remember one sentence from your talk, what would it be? That's your message. Everything else in the talk supports it.

This is the hardest discipline in speaking. You'll be tempted to share everything you know. Resist. The talks that land are the ones with one clear idea, hammered home from multiple angles.

### Lesson 5.3 — The classic structure

For most talks, this works:

1. **Hook** (30–60 seconds) — earn their attention
2. **Promise** (30 seconds) — what they'll get from listening
3. **Body** (most of the talk) — usually 3 sections, each with a story
4. **Land** (1 minute) — call back to opening, deliver the one big idea
5. **Call to action** (15–30 seconds) — what should they do now

### Lesson 5.4 — The rule of three (used well, not as a tic)

Humans love things in threes. Three points, three examples, three sections. It's memorable and feels complete.

But don't force everything into threes. If you have two great points, don't pad to three. If you have five, group them into three larger categories.

### Lesson 5.5 — Transitions matter

Audiences need help moving between sections. Verbal signposts:

- "That brings me to my second point..."
- "But here's what changed everything..."
- "Now you might be thinking..."
- "Let me tell you about the time..."

These aren't crutches. They're roadmaps. Use them.

### Lesson 5.6 — The ending

A weak ending kills the whole talk. Strong endings:

- **Callback** — reference something from your opening. Closes the loop.
- **One big statement** — a single line that delivers the message
- **A challenge** — leave the audience with something to do
- **A question** — leave them thinking
- **A short story** — emotional resonance

Never end with "and that's about it" or "any questions?" Plan your ending. Memorize it. Deliver it strong.

### Exercise 5.1 — Outline a 5-minute talk

Pick a topic you care about. Outline a 5-minute talk:
- Hook (one paragraph)
- One-sentence message
- Three sections, each with a story
- Landing

### Exercise 5.2 — Write three openings

For the same talk topic, write three different opening 60 seconds. Test which one would grab you most.

### Exercise 5.3 — Steal endings

Watch 5 TED talks. Write down the last 30 seconds of each. Notice the techniques. Borrow them.

## Module 6: Presentation Craft

### Lesson 6.1 — Slides are not the talk

The biggest mistake in school and corporate presentations: people read their slides to the audience. The audience can read. They don't need you to do it for them.

Slides should be a *visual aid* for what you're saying, not a teleprompter.

### Lesson 6.2 — Slide rules

1. **One idea per slide.** Don't pack 5 bullet points on each slide.
2. **Big text or no text.** If you must use words, use few words in big type. People shouldn't squint.
3. **Strong visuals.** A single powerful image often beats words entirely.
4. **No bullet point graveyards.** Long lists of bullets are slide death.
5. **Don't decorate.** Animations, transitions, weird fonts — all distract. Keep it clean.

### Lesson 6.3 — When NOT to use slides

If you don't need slides, don't use them. Some of the best talks have no slides at all — just a person, a story, and a microphone.

Use slides when:
- You're showing data that needs visualization
- You're showing images that support your point
- The audience benefits from seeing something while you talk

Skip slides when:
- You're telling a personal story
- You're delivering a short message
- The slides would just repeat what you're saying

### Lesson 6.4 — Speaking notes (or no notes)

Three options for notes:

1. **Memorized.** Best for short, important talks. Risk: sounds rehearsed. Practice until it sounds natural.

2. **Bullet outline.** A few keywords on cards or a tablet. Works for most talks. You speak naturally, glance at notes to stay on track.

3. **Fully scripted, read aloud.** Only works if you're skilled at reading naturally (most aren't). Avoid for short talks. Politicians use this, then it sounds like politicians talking.

For most speakers, the bullet outline is best. Memorize the opening, ending, and key story beats. Improvise the rest within the structure.

### Lesson 6.5 — Practice properly

Reading your talk silently doesn't count as practice.

**Real practice:**
1. Speak it out loud, all the way through
2. Record yourself, watch it back
3. Time yourself
4. Practice the transitions especially
5. Practice in the actual room if possible
6. Do it at least 5–10 times before the real thing

Yes, this is more practice than most people do. That's why most people give forgettable talks.

### Exercise 6.1 — Build a slide deck (or kill one)

Take an existing presentation you've made for school. Cut the number of slides in half. Cut the words per slide by 75%. Add one strong image per slide. Practice giving the talk with the new slides.

### Exercise 6.2 — The no-slide test

Take a topic you'd normally prepare slides for. Plan to deliver it with no slides at all. Notice what changes in your preparation. The talk usually gets better.

### Exercise 6.3 — Practice rep

Pick a 5-minute talk you've outlined. Practice it 5 times this week — out loud, standing up, in front of a mirror. Time each one. By rep 5, you'll be markedly better than rep 1.

## Module 7: Pitching and Persuading Live

### Lesson 7.1 — The pitch structure

When you're trying to get someone to say yes (to a partnership, investment, opportunity), use this:

1. **The hook** — one sentence that makes them care
2. **The problem** — what's wrong in the world
3. **The solution** — what you're offering
4. **The proof** — why this will work
5. **The ask** — what you specifically need

This works for 30-second elevator pitches, 5-minute investor pitches, 30-minute business proposals. Same structure, different depths.

### Lesson 7.2 — The 30-second pitch

You meet someone influential. They ask "What do you do?" You have 30 seconds.

**Bad answer:** "Well, I'm a student and I'm into a lot of things, basically I do music and writing and also I'm starting a business..."

**Good answer:** "I'm building a tutoring marketplace that connects WAEC students with university students who can teach them. We've got 200 tutors signed up and our first cohort starts in January."

Specific. Memorable. Easy to follow up on.

Write your 30-second pitch. Memorize it. Be able to deliver it cold.

### Lesson 7.3 — Reading the room

In live situations (not pre-prepared talks), you have to read the audience.

Signs you should adjust:
- They look bored → speed up, get more concrete
- They look confused → slow down, simplify
- They check phones → say something surprising or invite participation
- They're nodding and leaning in → keep going, you're hitting it

Don't pretend the audience isn't there. They're half the conversation.

### Lesson 7.4 — Handling questions

Q&A is when many speakers fall apart. Don't be one of them.

**Rules for handling questions:**

1. **Listen all the way.** Don't start answering before they finish.
2. **Repeat or reframe** if useful: "So your question is whether this works in rural areas — yes, here's why."
3. **It's okay to say "I don't know."** Then offer how you'd find out.
4. **Don't ramble.** Answer the question, then stop.
5. **Stay calm with hostile questions.** Don't get defensive. Treat them like genuine questions, even if they're attacks.

### Lesson 7.5 — Handling the hostile audience

Sometimes audiences are tough. They disagree, push back, or attack.

- **Don't fight back emotionally.** This loses every time.
- **Acknowledge the legitimate part of their concern.** Even hostile questions usually have a real concern underneath.
- **Stay grounded.** Don't let one tough question derail your whole talk.
- **Have prepared lines for common attacks.** Know what you'll say if someone says "this won't work because X."

### Lesson 7.6 — The follow-up

The talk doesn't end when you leave the stage. Strong speakers:

- Talk to people who approach them after
- Connect on LinkedIn or follow up on their cards
- Send thank-yous to people who organized or asked good questions
- Reflect on what worked, what didn't, what to improve

The follow-up is half the value of speaking. Don't skip it.

### Exercise 7.1 — Your 30-second pitch

Write your own 30-second pitch. Memorize it. Deliver it to 5 different people this week. Notice their reactions.

### Exercise 7.2 — Q&A practice

Give any 5-minute talk to a friend. Have them ask you 5 hard questions afterward. Practice answering clearly and briefly.

### Exercise 7.3 — The hostile question drill

For any topic you'd talk about, write down the 5 toughest questions someone could ask you. Now practice your answers. Going in prepared kills 90% of Q&A anxiety.

## Module 8: Capstone

### Capstone — The Real Talk

Over 4 weeks, prepare and deliver one real talk.

**Week 1: Design**
- Choose a topic you care about
- Define your one-sentence message
- Outline the structure
- Identify 3 personal stories that support the message

**Week 2: Draft**
- Write your hook (memorize this)
- Draft each section
- Write your ending (memorize this too)
- Run through the full talk out loud — see how long it is

**Week 3: Practice**
- Practice 5 times minimum
- Record yourself
- Watch yourself
- Adjust based on what you see

**Week 4: Deliver**
- Give the talk to a real audience of at least 5 people
- Could be: family gathering, school club, online live stream, community event
- Get feedback from at least 2 people afterward
- Reflect: what worked, what didn't, what's next?

**Deliverable:** A video of yourself giving the talk. Watch it. This is your baseline. Every future talk should be better than this one.
