**For learners 15–19 · Self-paced · ~15 hours**

This is the course nobody teaches in school but everyone wishes they'd learned. By the time you're 25, the ability to spot an opportunity, build something, sell it, and improve it will matter more than any degree on a wall.

This isn't theory. By the end of this course, you'll have built and sold something real, even if it's small. That experience is worth more than reading 50 business books.

## Module 1: The Entrepreneur Mindset

### Lesson 1.1 — Entrepreneurship is solving problems for money

Strip away all the hype. Entrepreneurship means: you notice someone has a problem, you solve it, they pay you. That's it.

Everything else — pitching, fundraising, scaling, branding — is downstream of that. If you can't solve a real problem for a real person who'll pay real money, none of the other stuff matters.

This means you don't need a "big idea." You need a small problem someone has and a willingness to solve it.

### Lesson 1.2 — Why teens have unfair advantages

Adults take teens less seriously. That sounds bad. It's actually an advantage.

- Nobody expects you to know everything → you can ask questions adults would be embarrassed to ask
- You can experiment cheaply → no rent, no kids, no overhead
- You have time to fail → 30 years to recover from any mistake
- People want to help young people who're trying → mentors, customers, even competitors will give you info they wouldn't give an adult
- You're closer to other young people → that's a market most adults don't understand

The successful adult entrepreneurs you read about? They mostly started young. The age you are right now is the best time to start.

### Lesson 1.3 — The two myths that kill teen entrepreneurs

**Myth 1: You need an original idea.** No, you don't. Most successful businesses copy something that already works and do it differently or better. WhatsApp wasn't the first messaging app. Facebook wasn't the first social network. Coca-Cola wasn't the first soda. Originality is overrated. Execution is everything.

**Myth 2: You need money to start.** Some businesses need capital. Most digital businesses don't. You can start with: a phone, an internet connection, and what you already know. Anyone who tells you otherwise is either lying or selling something.

### Lesson 1.4 — The traits that actually matter

Forget "passion" and "vision." The actual traits of successful young entrepreneurs:

1. **Resourcefulness** — figuring things out instead of being stuck
2. **Persistence** — keeping going when something doesn't work the first time
3. **Customer focus** — caring more about solving problems than being clever
4. **Comfort with rejection** — most people will say no; that's normal
5. **Willingness to ask** — for help, for advice, for sales

You'll develop these by doing, not by reading about them.

### Exercise 1.1 — The problem journal

For the next 7 days, write down every time you notice someone (including yourself) complaining about something. "Transport is so expensive." "I can't find a good tutor for my brother." "There's nowhere good to print study materials near my school." 

Each complaint is a potential business opportunity. By the end of the week you'll have 20–50 problems written down. Some will be tiny. Some will be big. Some will be opportunities.

### Exercise 1.2 — The capability inventory

List 30 things you can do. Don't filter. Include obvious ones (use Canva, write essays, speak French) and weird ones (find good Instagram captions, organize messy spaces, calm crying babies). 

This is your raw material. Most of these will combine with problems from Exercise 1.1 to create business ideas.

## Module 2: Finding Real Opportunities

### Lesson 2.1 — Ideas vs opportunities

An **idea** is something that sounds cool to you.

An **opportunity** is a real problem real people have, that you can solve, and they'd pay for.

Most "business ideas" aren't opportunities. They're solutions in search of a problem. The graveyard of failed startups is full of clever ideas nobody actually wanted.

The test: can you name 10 specific people (not "young women aged 18–35") who would benefit from this and might pay? If not, you have an idea, not an opportunity.

### Lesson 2.2 — Where opportunities hide

The best opportunities are hiding in plain sight:

**Friction.** Things that take too long, cost too much, or hurt to do. Wherever people are wasting time or money, opportunity exists. (Lagos traffic created Bolt and Opay. Bad banking in Nigeria created the entire fintech industry.)

**Recurring complaints.** When you hear the same complaint from 5 different people, that's a signal.

**Things you do yourself.** What annoys you that you've had to figure out? Other people probably have the same problem.

**Adjacent markets.** Something that works in Lagos might not exist in Abeokuta yet. Something that works in the US might not have a local version.

**Underserved niches.** Big companies ignore small markets. Small markets can support small businesses.

### Lesson 2.3 — Opportunity scoring

Not all opportunities are equal. Score each one on:

1. **Problem intensity** — how painful is this for the person? (1–10)
2. **Market size** — how many people have this problem? (small/medium/large)
3. **Your ability** — can you actually deliver the solution? (1–10)
4. **Competition** — how crowded is the space? (low/medium/high)
5. **Willingness to pay** — do people pay for solutions like this already? (yes/maybe/no)

A great first business has: high problem intensity + medium market + high ability + low competition + clear willingness to pay.

### Lesson 2.4 — Digital opportunities for African teens

The internet collapses distance. From your phone in Lagos, you can:

- **Sell digital products globally** — templates, courses, ebooks, prompts
- **Sell services to international clients** — design, writing, social media, coding
- **Sell to other African teens** — study materials, tutoring, content
- **Resell physical products via Instagram/WhatsApp** — phone accessories, fashion, beauty
- **Build content audiences** — TikTok, YouTube, Substack
- **Create localized versions** of things that exist elsewhere

### Exercise 2.1 — Opportunity scoring

Take the 20 problems from Module 1, Exercise 1.1. Score each one on the 5 dimensions in Lesson 2.3. Highlight the top 3.

### Exercise 2.2 — The interview round

Pick your top opportunity. Find 5 people who have this problem. Talk to each of them for 10 minutes. Ask:

- How big a problem is this for you, really?
- What have you tried so far?
- How much money or time does this cost you?
- If someone solved this, would you pay for it? How much?

You'll either confirm the opportunity is real or discover it isn't. Both are useful.

## Module 3: Validating Before Building

### Lesson 3.1 — The most expensive mistake

The classic teen mistake: spend 3 months building a product, then try to sell it, then discover no one wants it.

The right approach: spend 1 week proving people want it, then build it.

This is called **validation** — testing demand before building supply.

### Lesson 3.2 — The minimum viable test

Before you build anything, test the demand with the smallest possible thing.

Want to start a tutoring business? Don't build a website. Post on WhatsApp: "Offering ₦3,000/hour math tutoring for SS1–SS3 students. DM if interested." See who responds.

Want to sell digital templates? Don't build a Selar store. Post one template on Twitter with a Gumroad link. See if anyone buys.

Want to start a course? Don't film 20 hours of video. Sell a 1-hour live workshop first. See if people pay for the topic at all.

### Lesson 3.3 — Pre-selling

The strongest validation: people pay you before the thing exists.

This sounds risky but it's how the best businesses start.

"I'm planning to launch a course on [topic] in March. Early-bird price is 40% off if you sign up now. The course covers [X, Y, Z]. If you're interested, send ₦5,000 to lock in your spot."

If 10 people pay, you have validation AND startup capital. If nobody pays, you saved yourself months of building the wrong thing.

### Lesson 3.4 — Landing page tests

A **landing page** is a single web page describing what you're offering. You can build one in an hour using free tools like Carrd, Notion, or Linktree-style pages.

Test process:
1. Write a clear page describing what you'd offer, who it's for, what it costs
2. Add an email signup or pre-order button
3. Share it with your network (WhatsApp, Twitter, Instagram)
4. See how many people sign up or pre-order

The numbers tell you whether to build it.

### Lesson 3.5 — When to ignore validation

Sometimes you have to build something for people to even understand what it is. Steve Jobs famously said customers don't know what they want until you show them.

But for most teen businesses, validation is critical. You're not Steve Jobs (yet). Test before you build.

### Exercise 3.1 — The pre-sell

Pick your top opportunity from Module 2. Write a description of what you'd offer and what it costs. Post it on your social media or send to people who you think might want it. Goal: get at least one paid commitment within 14 days.

### Exercise 3.2 — Build a landing page

Use Carrd (free) or Notion. Build a one-page description of your idea. Include:
- The problem
- Your solution
- Who it's for
- What it costs
- How to buy

Share it with at least 10 people. Track who clicks, who signs up.

### Exercise 3.3 — The 5-customer interview

Once you have validation, talk to your first 5 actual or potential customers in depth. Use what they tell you to shape what you build.

## Module 4: Building Your First Offering

### Lesson 4.1 — Service vs product vs digital

You have three basic options for what to sell:

**Service** — you do something for someone. (Tutoring, design, writing, coaching, video editing.) Low startup cost. Trades time for money. Hard to scale.

**Physical product** — you sell stuff. (Reselling, your own products, food.) Inventory costs. Logistics. Margins matter.

**Digital product** — something you make once, sell many times. (Courses, ebooks, templates, prompts, presets, software.) Highest scaling potential. Most work to set up.

For first-time teen entrepreneurs, start with **services** or **simple digital products**. Both have low startup costs and let you learn how business actually works.

### Lesson 4.2 — Building a service offering

Three steps:

1. **Define the service narrowly.** Not "I'm a tutor." But "I help SS3 students prepare for JAMB English in 8 weeks."

2. **Define what the customer gets.** Number of sessions, duration, format, materials, results.

3. **Define what you don't do.** "I tutor English only — not other subjects. Sessions are 1 hour. I don't do homework for students."

Narrow services sell better than broad ones. "Math tutor" is vague. "JAMB Math prep for ALevel students aiming for engineering" sounds specific and valuable.

### Lesson 4.3 — Building a simple digital product

Easiest first digital products for teens:

**Notion templates** — study planners, habit trackers, project management for students. Sell on Gumroad.

**Canva templates** — Instagram templates for small businesses, presentation templates, CV templates. Sell on Etsy or Selar.

**Study guides / cheat sheets** — for specific subjects or exams. Sell on WhatsApp or your own site.

**Prompt packs** — AI prompts for specific use cases (study, content creation, business). Big opportunity right now.

**Ebooks** — short, specific, useful. Not 200 pages of fluff. 30 pages of real value.

**Spreadsheet templates** — budgets, business plans, trackers. Useful and easy to make.

### Lesson 4.4 — Where to sell digital products

**Global platforms:**
- **Gumroad** — easiest entry for digital products, takes credit cards globally
- **Selar** — Nigerian-friendly, supports naira payment
- **Paystack Storefront** — for Nigerian customers
- **Etsy** — for templates and printables
- **Whop** — for digital products and communities

**Direct sales:** Just message customers, send them the file, get paid via bank transfer or mobile money. Old-school but works for the first 10–20 sales.

### Lesson 4.5 — Delivering excellence on the first sale

Your first 10 customers are everything. They become testimonials, referrals, repeat buyers. Treat them like royalty.

For each one:
- Over-deliver on quality
- Communicate fast and clearly
- Ask for feedback after
- Ask for a testimonial if they're happy
- Ask if they know anyone else who'd benefit

This is how a one-person business grows for the first year.

### Exercise 4.1 — Define your offer

Write down your offering in this format:
- I help [specific audience]
- Solve [specific problem]
- By [specific method]
- In [specific timeframe]
- For [specific price]

If any of those blanks are vague, sharpen them.

### Exercise 4.2 — Build the smallest version

Build the simplest possible version of what you're selling. If it's a service: write your service description. If it's a digital product: make a basic version of the file.

Don't aim for perfect. Aim for usable.

### Exercise 4.3 — Get your first sale

Within 14 days of starting this module: make one actual sale. Charge real money, even if it's small. Document everything: who bought, where they came from, what they said.

## Module 5: Sales — The Most Important Skill

### Lesson 5.1 — Why builders hate sales (and why you can't)

Most teens who start businesses love building. They make beautiful products and then... wait. They post once on Instagram and wonder why nobody bought.

Building without selling = expensive hobby. Selling without building = scam. You need both. But sales is the rarer skill, so most businesses fail from lack of selling, not lack of product.

If you only learn one thing from this course, learn this: **selling is just helping people buy.** It's not pushy. It's not slimy. It's removing friction so someone who already has a problem can pay you to solve it.

### Lesson 5.2 — The mindset shift

Bad mindset: "I'm bothering people if I try to sell to them."

Good mindset: "If I genuinely have something that helps them, I'm bothering them if I *don't* tell them about it."

You're not asking for favors. You're offering value. The transaction is fair.

### Lesson 5.3 — The basic sales conversation

This is the core script for selling anything:

1. **Understand their situation.** Ask questions. Listen. ("Tell me about your current situation with X.")

2. **Identify the problem clearly.** Make sure they understand what's not working. ("So it sounds like the main issue is...")

3. **Show how your solution helps specifically.** Not features — outcomes. ("With what I offer, you'd be able to...")

4. **Handle objections honestly.** Price, time, doubt — address them directly. Don't dodge.

5. **Ask for the sale.** This is where most people freeze. Say it clearly: "Would you like to move forward?"

This works for selling tutoring, design services, courses, anything.

### Lesson 5.4 — Cold outreach (DMs that work)

When you're starting out and have no audience, you'll need to cold-message people.

**Bad cold DM:**
> Hi sir, I am a graphic designer and I will design your logo at a very affordable price. Kindly check my work and let me know.

That's 100 other DMs that person got this morning.

**Good cold DM:**
> Hi [name], I saw your post about [specific thing they posted]. I noticed [specific observation about their brand/page/situation]. I help [specific type of person] with [specific problem]. If [specific result] would be useful for your business, I'd love to share an idea I have for you (no obligation). Let me know.

The good DM is specific, useful, and doesn't ask for anything immediately. Send 20 of these per week and you'll get clients.

### Lesson 5.5 — Handling objections

Common objections and how to handle them:

**"It's too expensive."**
Don't drop the price immediately. Ask: "Compared to what?" Or: "What would make it worth it for you?" Often the real objection isn't price — it's that they're not convinced of the value yet.

**"I need to think about it."**
Ask: "What specifically do you need to think about?" This either surfaces the real concern or accepts a yes-or-no follow-up date.

**"I don't have the money right now."**
Ask: "When would be a better time?" Or offer a payment plan if appropriate. Or ask if there's a smaller version you could start with.

**"I'll get back to you."**
Set a specific follow-up date: "Sounds good — can I check back with you on Friday?"

### Lesson 5.6 — Follow-up is everything

Most sales aren't made on the first contact. They're made on the third, fifth, or eighth.

People forget. They get distracted. They need reminding. Following up isn't pestering — it's professional.

Rules:
- Follow up at least 3 times before giving up
- Space them out (day 1, day 3, day 7)
- Each follow-up should add something (a new thought, a relevant example, an offer)
- Don't follow up with just "any updates?" — be substantive

### Lesson 5.7 — Selling without lying

The line that should never be crossed: don't say things that aren't true.

- Don't fake reviews
- Don't claim results you didn't deliver
- Don't promise outcomes you can't guarantee
- Don't hide important information

Build a reputation as someone whose word is gold. This compounds for life.

### Exercise 5.1 — The 10 cold DMs

This week, send 10 personalized cold DMs to potential customers using the template in Lesson 5.4. Track responses.

### Exercise 5.2 — The objection script

Write out your responses to the 5 most likely objections you'll hear in your business. Practice them out loud so you don't freeze when they come up.

### Exercise 5.3 — The follow-up system

Set up a simple system (Google Sheets is fine) to track conversations with potential customers. Columns: Name, Last Contact, Next Follow-up Date, Notes. Use it for the next month.

## Module 6: Pricing and Packaging

### Lesson 6.1 — Most teens underprice

The classic mistake: charging too little to attract customers. This backfires in three ways:

1. You attract the worst customers (the ones who only care about price)
2. You can't afford to deliver quality
3. People assume cheap = low value, so they don't trust you

Charging higher prices isn't greedy. It's necessary for sustaining a real business.

### Lesson 6.2 — How to price a service

Three pricing methods:

**Cost-plus pricing.** Calculate your costs (time, materials), add your desired profit. Simple but undervalues you.

**Competitor-based pricing.** Look at what others charge for similar services, position above or below. Quick but lazy.

**Value-based pricing.** Charge based on the outcome you create for the customer. Hard but most powerful.

For a teen starting out: research competitor prices. Charge 70–80% of mid-range as a beginner. Raise prices as you get better and busier.

### Lesson 6.3 — Pricing tiers

Don't offer just one price. Offer 3 tiers (basic / standard / premium). Most customers will pick the middle. Tiers anchor expectations.

**Example for tutoring:**
- Basic: ₦3,000/hour, single session
- Standard: ₦15,000 for 5-session package (saves ₦0)
- Premium: ₦40,000 for 10-session package + WhatsApp support between sessions

The middle option is the one most people pick. It's also usually the most profitable per hour for you.

### Lesson 6.4 — Pricing digital products

Digital products have near-zero marginal cost (one extra copy doesn't cost you anything). So pricing depends on value to customer, not cost to you.

Rules:
- Start higher than feels comfortable
- Have a real reason for the price
- Anchor it against alternatives ("compared to a ₦100,000 course, this ₦15,000 cheat sheet gets you 80% of the value")
- Use scarcity sparingly (early-bird pricing, limited cohort) — don't fake it

### Lesson 6.5 — Raising prices

Every 3–6 months, evaluate your prices. If you're consistently busy, raise prices. If you're getting better at what you do, raise prices. If your costs are going up (inflation, expenses), raise prices.

How to raise prices:
1. Notify existing customers in advance (often you can keep them at old rates as loyalty)
2. Apply new prices to new customers immediately
3. Don't apologize. Just announce.

The customers who push back hardest are usually the ones you don't want anyway.

### Lesson 6.6 — Free isn't a strategy (usually)

"Free" attracts everyone. Which means it attracts the wrong people for your business. Free trials, free samples, free consultations can work — but as paths to paid offers, not as the main offering.

Exception: if you're building an audience first (content, social), free is the goal. But that's a different business model.

### Exercise 6.1 — Build your price tiers

Design 3 pricing tiers for your offering. Make sure each one is clearly different in value, not just price.

### Exercise 6.2 — Competitor research

Find 5 competitors selling similar offerings. List their prices, what's included, and how they position themselves. Use this to position yourself.

### Exercise 6.3 — Raise prices

If you've made some sales already: raise your price by 25% on the next customer. Notice if anything changes.

## Module 7: Marketing for the Broke

### Lesson 7.1 — Marketing isn't ads

Most teens think marketing = paying for Instagram ads. That's the worst kind of marketing for a new business with no proof.

Real marketing is everything that makes potential customers know you, like you, and trust you. Most of it is free.

### Lesson 7.2 — The five free marketing channels

**1. Content creation.** Post regularly on platforms your customers use. Show your work, share lessons, teach what you know. The goal is to become known for something specific.

**2. Cold outreach.** Direct messages to potential customers. Slow but effective.

**3. Referrals.** Existing customers telling others. This is the highest-converting source of new customers.

**4. Community presence.** Be active in WhatsApp groups, Discord servers, Reddit threads, Twitter spaces where your customers hang out. Help people. Don't sell. People notice helpful people.

**5. Collaborations.** Team up with someone who has the audience you want. Cross-promote each other. Both win.

### Lesson 7.3 — Content marketing for teens

Pick ONE platform first. Don't try to be on all of them.

**TikTok / Instagram Reels / YouTube Shorts** — best for reach. Teens watch a lot of short video. If your customers are teens or young adults, this is the place.

**Twitter/X** — best for building reputation in professional/business spaces. If your customers are adults in business or tech, here.

**LinkedIn** — best for B2B selling (selling to businesses). If your customers are professionals or company decision-makers, here.

**YouTube** (long-form) — best for in-depth audience building. Slower to grow but more loyal.

**WhatsApp Status / Channels** — local reach in Africa. Underrated.

Whatever you pick, post consistently. 3 times a week minimum, for at least 6 months, before judging if it's working.

### Lesson 7.4 — What to post

The three-bucket content strategy:

1. **Teach** (40%) — show what you know. Solve small problems for free.
2. **Show your work** (30%) — behind-the-scenes, what you're building, what you're learning.
3. **Be a person** (30%) — your story, your opinions, your daily life. People follow people, not brands.

What NOT to post much:
- Endless promo for your product
- Generic motivational quotes
- Repurposed memes with no commentary
- Apologies for not posting more

### Lesson 7.5 — The referral system

Referrals are the most valuable marketing channel. Build a system:

1. **Deliver excellence first.** Bad service doesn't get referred.
2. **Ask explicitly.** Most happy customers won't refer unless you ask. "If you know anyone else who might benefit from this, I'd love an intro."
3. **Make it easy.** Give them a clear message they can forward, a landing page link, a referral code.
4. **Reward them.** Discount on their next purchase, free upgrade, even just a personal thank-you. Reciprocity is real.

### Lesson 7.6 — When to spend on ads

Only spend on ads when:
1. You've made at least 50 sales from organic marketing
2. You know what type of customer pays well
3. You have a clear conversion path (ad → landing page → sale)
4. You can afford to lose the ad money testing

Until then, all your marketing should be free or based on time, not money.

### Exercise 7.1 — Pick your channel

Choose ONE social platform to focus on for 6 months. Write down why you picked it, who your audience is there, and what kind of content you'll post.

### Exercise 7.2 — The 30-day content sprint

For 30 days, post 3x per week on your chosen platform. At the end, review: which posts got engagement? Which got customers? Adjust.

### Exercise 7.3 — Get one referral

Ask 3 happy customers for referrals this week. Track who actually delivers one. Thank them properly.

## Module 8: Capstone — Launch Something

### The Capstone Project

Over 8 weeks, you will launch a real business and make real sales. Not a school project. Not a hypothetical. A real thing that real people pay you for.

**Weeks 1–2: Validate**
- Pick your opportunity (from earlier modules)
- Interview 5 potential customers
- Get at least one verbal commitment to buy

**Weeks 3–4: Build**
- Create the simplest version of your offering
- Build a one-page description (Notion, Carrd, or Selar)
- Set your pricing (with 3 tiers)

**Weeks 5–6: Sell**
- Make your first 5 sales
- Use cold outreach + your network + content
- Track every conversation

**Weeks 7–8: Deliver and improve**
- Deliver excellent work to your first customers
- Get feedback
- Get 2 testimonials
- Get 1 referral
- Plan your next 30 days

### Capstone Deliverable

A document containing:
- Your offering (what, who, why, how much)
- Your sales numbers (how many, how much revenue, profit margin)
- Customer testimonials
- What you learned (3–5 key insights)
- Your 90-day plan to grow

Don't quit the business after the course ends. Most businesses that survive past 6 months survive for years. Get past the 6-month mark.
