## Module 1: The Thinking Gap

### Lesson 1.1 — Why most people don't think well

Clear thinking is hard. Your brain didn't evolve for accuracy — it evolved for survival on the savanna. Fast pattern matching, social belonging, threat detection. These were useful then. They cause problems now.

Your brain runs on autopilot most of the time. It's why advertising works. It's why political propaganda works. It's why scams work. It's why smart people believe stupid things.

Critical thinking is the deliberate effort to override autopilot when accuracy matters. It's tiring. It's slow. It's worth it.

### Lesson 1.2 — Fast and slow thinking

Psychologist Daniel Kahneman distinguishes:

**System 1** — fast, intuitive, emotional, automatic. You use it when driving familiar roads, recognizing faces, reacting to "obvious" things.

**System 2** — slow, deliberate, effortful, logical. You use it when doing math, planning, evaluating evidence.

System 1 handles 95% of your decisions. That's fine for most things. The problem: System 1 also handles many things it shouldn't — important decisions about money, beliefs, relationships, politics. These need System 2.

The skill is recognizing when to slow down and engage System 2 deliberately.

### Lesson 1.3 — The four enemies of clear thinking

When you can't think clearly, it's usually one of these:

**Emotion.** Strong feelings hijack reasoning. Anger, fear, attachment to identity.

**Tribe.** What your group believes feels true regardless of evidence. Disagreeing feels like betrayal.

**Speed.** Quick reactions favor System 1. Most online environments push you to react fast.

**Effort.** Real thinking is mentally tiring. Avoiding it is the default.

When stakes matter, all four of these are working against you. Knowing this is your first defense.

### Lesson 1.4 — The good news

Clear thinking is a skill. Skills can be learned. Most adults never bother because school doesn't teach it well and life doesn't punish you for being bad at it (until it does).

A teen who deliberately works on this for a year will out-think 90% of adults by 19. Not because they're smarter — because they practiced.

### Exercise 1.1 — Catch yourself

For one day, notice every time you have a strong opinion about something. Ask yourself: am I responding from autopilot or from actual thinking? Most of the time, it'll be autopilot. That's the data.

### Exercise 1.2 — The strong belief test

Pick a strong belief you hold. Ask:
- Where did this belief come from?
- What evidence supports it?
- What would change my mind?
- Have I ever seriously considered the opposite?

If you can't answer these, the belief is unexamined. That doesn't mean it's wrong, just that you don't actually know if it's right.

## Module 2: Logic and Reasoning Basics

### Lesson 2.1 — What a good argument looks like

A logical argument has:

1. **Premises** — claims you're starting from
2. **Reasoning** — how the premises lead to the conclusion
3. **Conclusion** — what you're trying to prove

For an argument to be sound:
- The premises must be true
- The reasoning must be valid

If either fails, the argument fails.

### Lesson 2.2 — Deductive reasoning

**Deductive** reasoning moves from general principles to specific conclusions.

> All humans are mortal. (premise)
> Adaeze is human. (premise)
> Therefore, Adaeze is mortal. (conclusion)

If the premises are true and the logic is valid, the conclusion is guaranteed.

Most everyday reasoning isn't this clean. But the structure is useful.

### Lesson 2.3 — Inductive reasoning

**Inductive** reasoning moves from specific observations to general conclusions.

> I've seen 50 swans, and all of them were white.
> Therefore, all swans are white.

Inductive conclusions aren't guaranteed. They're probable. Until you see your first black swan (which exists in Australia), and then the conclusion turns out to be wrong.

Most science, most everyday knowledge, works this way. You should always hold inductive conclusions with appropriate uncertainty.

### Lesson 2.4 — Common logical fallacies

A **fallacy** is a flaw in reasoning. Some of the most common ones:

**Ad hominem.** Attacking the person instead of the argument.
> "You can't trust her opinion on economics — she's only 17."
The person's age doesn't make their argument wrong. Engage the argument.

**Strawman.** Misrepresenting someone's argument to make it easier to attack.
> "You support reducing screen time? So you think kids should never use phones?"
Nobody said never. The strawman makes the opposing position seem extreme.

**Appeal to authority.** Believing something because an authority says so, without engaging the argument.
> "This famous person said X, so X must be true."
Authorities can be wrong. Especially outside their area of expertise.

**Appeal to popularity.** Believing something because many people believe it.
> "Everyone agrees we should..."
Most people once believed the earth was flat. Popularity isn't proof.

**False dilemma.** Presenting two options as if they're the only choices.
> "Either you're with us or against us."
Usually there are more than two options.

**Slippery slope.** Assuming one step will inevitably lead to extreme consequences without showing why.
> "If we let students choose their lunch, soon they'll be making all the rules."
Maybe. Maybe not. The argument needs evidence, not just assumption.

**Circular reasoning.** Using your conclusion as a premise.
> "The Bible is true because the Bible says it's true."
This doesn't establish anything. The conclusion needs independent support.

**Genetic fallacy.** Judging an idea by where it came from rather than its merit.
> "That idea came from a bad person, so it must be wrong."
A clock that's broken is right twice a day. Even unpleasant sources can have valid points.

**Whataboutism.** Deflecting criticism by pointing to someone else's behavior.
> "Yes, our country has corruption, but what about Country X?"
Even if Country X is worse, that doesn't address the original issue.

**Hasty generalization.** Drawing broad conclusions from too few examples.
> "I met two rude people from Lagos, so Lagosians are rude."
Two people don't represent a city of 20 million.

### Lesson 2.5 — Recognizing fallacies

Knowing fallacy names doesn't make you smarter. Recognizing them when they happen does.

In conversation, when something feels off about an argument, ask:
- Are they attacking the person or the argument?
- Are they describing my position fairly?
- Are they treating popularity or authority as proof?
- Are they presenting false either-or choices?

You'll start seeing fallacies everywhere. In ads. In politics. In your own reasoning. That last one matters most.

### Exercise 2.1 — Fallacy hunting

Open social media or any opinion article. Find 5 examples of logical fallacies. Identify which type each one is.

### Exercise 2.2 — Self-fallacy audit

Think of an argument you've made recently in conversation or online. Honestly examine it — did you use any fallacies? Most people do, sometimes. The point isn't shame; it's awareness.

### Exercise 2.3 — Rebuild your argument

Take a position you hold. Write it out as: premise 1, premise 2, conclusion. Check the structure. Is each premise actually true? Does the conclusion really follow? Most people have never done this with their own beliefs.

## Module 3: Cognitive Biases — Your Brain's Bugs

### Lesson 3.1 — Why we have biases

Biases are mental shortcuts your brain uses to save energy. They mostly work. When they don't, they produce systematic errors in thinking.

You can't eliminate your biases. They're built in. But you can recognize them when they show up and override them when accuracy matters.

### Lesson 3.2 — Confirmation bias

The big one. Your brain seeks information that confirms what you already believe and ignores or dismisses information that challenges it.

This is why two people can read the same news article and walk away with opposite takeaways. Each saw what fit their existing view.

How to counter:
- Deliberately seek out the strongest arguments against your view
- Read sources you disagree with
- Notice when you're dismissing information because it conflicts with what you believe
- Ask "what would change my mind?" — and mean it

### Lesson 3.3 — Anchoring

The first number or fact you encounter shapes everything that follows.

If I tell you "this phone normally costs ₦300,000" before showing you the ₦150,000 price, the deal feels great. If I just showed you ₦150,000, you'd evaluate it on its own merit.

Salespeople use this constantly. So do negotiators. So do politicians.

How to counter:
- Notice when a number is being thrown out first
- Ask "is this anchor relevant or just designed to influence me?"
- Make your own estimates before hearing others'

### Lesson 3.4 — Availability bias

You overestimate things that come easily to mind. Plane crashes feel common (memorable, vivid). Car crashes feel routine (less memorable, more common).

This is why you're scared of shark attacks and not falling vending machines, even though the latter kills more people.

How to counter:
- Distrust your intuitions about probability
- Look up actual rates before making judgments
- Notice when your fear is driven by recent news, not real risk

### Lesson 3.5 — Survivorship bias

You see the winners. You don't see the losers. So you draw wrong conclusions.

"Most successful entrepreneurs dropped out of college" — but only successful ones. The thousands who dropped out and failed are invisible.

"This investing strategy made millions for these people" — but how many used it and lost everything?

How to counter:
- Always ask "what's missing from this picture?"
- Look for the failures and forgotten cases
- Be especially skeptical of success stories that imply a formula

### Lesson 3.6 — In-group bias

You judge people in your group more favorably than outsiders. Your tribe seems smarter, kinder, more reasonable. Other tribes seem flawed.

Your tribe might be your school, ethnic group, political side, fandom, religion, nationality, whatever.

How to counter:
- Apply the same standards to your group as others
- Specifically look for failings in your group and strengths in opposing ones
- When your group does something wrong, name it honestly

### Lesson 3.7 — The Dunning-Kruger effect

People who know little about a topic often have inflated confidence. People who know a lot are often more humble — they know what they don't know.

This is why social media is full of people confidently wrong about complex topics. And why experts often sound uncertain.

How to counter:
- Ask yourself: how much do I actually know about this?
- Distrust extreme confidence on complex topics
- Be willing to say "I don't know" — it's a sign of intellectual maturity

### Lesson 3.8 — Sunk cost fallacy

You don't quit something because of how much you've already invested in it, even when continuing isn't rational.

You're 200 pages into a book you hate. You finish it. Why? Because of the time already spent. But continuing only adds more wasted time.

You've been studying for a career for 3 years and realize you hate it. You continue. Why? Sunk cost.

How to counter:
- Ask: "If I were starting from now, would I make this choice?"
- The past is gone. Decisions should be made on future value.

### Lesson 3.9 — The Bandwagon effect

What people around you believe seems true. What everyone is doing seems right.

This is sometimes useful (don't run into traffic when others don't). It's sometimes dangerous (entire societies have believed terrible things together).

How to counter:
- Separate "everyone believes this" from "this is true"
- Be willing to be the only one in the room with a different view
- Ask: "If I weren't surrounded by people who believed X, would I still believe X?"

### Exercise 3.1 — Your bias inventory

Pick 3 biases from this module. For each, find a specific recent example from your own thinking. Most people can find examples within an hour of looking.

### Exercise 3.2 — The opposite view week

For one week, deliberately read or watch content from a perspective you disagree with. Take it seriously. Look for what they get right. Most people have never done this. You'll learn more in this week than in a year of confirming your existing views.

### Exercise 3.3 — The confidence check

For the next week, when you make a confident claim about something, ask yourself: how much do I actually know about this? Notice how often confidence outpaces knowledge.

## Module 4: Media Literacy in 2026

### Lesson 4.1 — The information environment you live in

You're growing up in the most information-saturated period in human history. Some of that information is true. Some is false. Most is somewhere in between — partially true, missing context, biased in ways that aren't always obvious.

Add AI to this. Now anyone can generate convincing fake text, images, videos, audio. The amount of synthetic content online is growing every month.

Without media literacy, you're a sitting duck. With it, you have a fighting chance.

### Lesson 4.2 — Types of bad information

**Misinformation** — false information shared without intent to deceive. People genuinely believe what they're spreading.

**Disinformation** — false information shared deliberately to deceive. Often for political, financial, or ideological reasons.

**Mal-information** — true information shared to harm someone. Like leaking real private information to hurt someone.

**Propaganda** — information (true or false) designed to advance a specific agenda, often emotional rather than factual.

You'll encounter all four. Recognizing what type something is helps you decide how to respond.

### Lesson 4.3 — How false information spreads

Bad information doesn't spread because it's true. It spreads because it's:

- Emotional (outrage, fear, shock)
- Shareable (short, visual, simple)
- Tribal (confirms what your group believes)
- Urgent (you feel you have to respond NOW)

Any time you feel a strong urge to share something immediately, that's the moment to slow down and check. The strongest emotional content is the most often false.

### Lesson 4.4 — The SIFT method

When you see information, run these four steps:

**S — Stop.** Don't react immediately. Don't share. Pause.

**I — Investigate the source.** Who's behind this? What's their track record? What's their motivation?

**F — Find better coverage.** Search the topic. What do multiple reputable sources say? Are they corroborating or contradicting?

**T — Trace claims to original sources.** Most online claims are summaries of summaries. Find the original study, document, or footage.

SIFT takes 2 minutes. It catches most misinformation before you spread it.

### Lesson 4.5 — Fact-checking tools

Use these to verify claims:

- **Snopes** — long-running fact checker
- **AfricaCheck** — fact-checking focused on African claims (Nigerian, South African, Kenyan offices)
- **AFP Fact Check** — global fact-checking
- **PolitiFact** — US-focused but rigorous
- **Reverse image search** (Google Images, TinEye) — see where an image actually comes from
- **InVID** — verify videos
- **Wayback Machine** (archive.org) — see if a webpage's claim was different earlier

For African contexts specifically, AfricaCheck is invaluable. They've debunked countless false claims about everything from health to politics to economics.

### Lesson 4.6 — Deepfakes and synthetic media

AI can now generate:

- Photos of people who don't exist
- Videos of real people saying things they never said
- Voice recordings impersonating anyone
- Entire articles that read perfectly

The technology is improving fast. By 2027, the average person won't be able to tell deepfakes from reality without specialized tools.

What you can do:

- Verify shocking content through trusted sources before believing
- If a public figure "said" something outrageous, check if reputable outlets are covering it
- Cross-reference unusual claims with multiple independent sources
- Be most skeptical of content designed to outrage you

### Lesson 4.7 — The algorithm shapes what you see

Every social media platform shows you content their algorithm thinks will keep you engaged. Engagement ≠ truth. Often engagement comes from outrage, controversy, or extreme views.

This means:

- Your feed is curated to be addictive, not informative
- You're not seeing a representative slice of opinion
- Whatever you engage with, you'll see more of
- The most extreme version of any view is what tends to spread

You can fight back:
- Use different sources, not just social media
- Read directly from primary sources sometimes
- Notice when an algorithm is feeding your outrage
- Follow people you disagree with to break the bubble

### Lesson 4.8 — The headline problem

Headlines often misrepresent the article. They're written to generate clicks, not accuracy.

A study that shows a modest effect becomes "X Causes Y!" in the headline. A nuanced political situation becomes "Crisis!" or "Triumph!" depending on the outlet.

Rule: never share an article you haven't read past the headline. The body often contradicts or seriously qualifies the headline.

### Exercise 4.1 — Apply SIFT

Pick the next 3 surprising things you see on social media this week. Run SIFT on each one before sharing. Notice how often the content doesn't survive investigation.

### Exercise 4.2 — Reverse image search

Find a viral image on social media. Run a reverse image search. Often you'll discover the image is old, miscaptioned, or from a different event entirely.

### Exercise 4.3 — The diet test

For one week, deliberately add 3 sources you usually don't read. Different political perspectives, different countries, different types of media. Notice how your view of "what's happening" changes.

## Module 5: Evaluating Sources

### Lesson 5.1 — Why "trust the experts" isn't enough

You'll hear "trust the experts" as advice. It's partially right. Experts know things. But experts are also:

- Sometimes wrong
- Influenced by funding and incentives
- Limited to their specific area
- Sometimes disagreeing with each other

The right approach isn't "trust everything experts say" or "trust nothing experts say." It's developing the skill to evaluate which experts on which topics in which contexts.

### Lesson 5.2 — Source quality factors

When evaluating any source, ask:

**Expertise.** Does this person/outlet have actual knowledge of this topic? Credentials, experience, track record.

**Track record.** Have they been right or wrong on similar claims in the past?

**Incentives.** What do they gain from this position? Money, status, ideology, tribal loyalty?

**Method.** How did they reach this conclusion? Evidence, reasoning, just feelings?

**Transparency.** Do they show their work? Cite sources? Acknowledge uncertainty?

**Independence.** Are they beholden to a specific funder, party, or interest?

A source that scores well on most of these factors deserves weight. One that scores poorly should be treated cautiously, no matter how confidently they speak.

### Lesson 5.3 — The reliability hierarchy

Roughly from most to least reliable for factual questions:

1. **Meta-analyses and systematic reviews** — synthesizing many studies
2. **Peer-reviewed scientific journals** — but recognize replication crisis
3. **Major reputable news outlets with editorial standards** — Reuters, AP, BBC, Le Monde, etc.
4. **Specialized journalism** — outlets covering specific beats expertly
5. **Academic books from established publishers**
6. **General news outlets** — variable quality
7. **Magazines and opinion outlets** — fine for opinion, less for facts
8. **Wikipedia** — usually right, sometimes wrong, always verify before citing
9. **Mainstream social media accounts** — varies wildly
10. **Anonymous social media posts** — very low default trust

This isn't perfect. Major newspapers sometimes get things wrong. Random social media accounts sometimes break true stories. But the priors should guide you.

### Lesson 5.4 — Evaluating scientific claims

For health, science, and academic claims:

- Has the study been replicated?
- How large was the sample?
- Was it a meta-analysis (strong) or single study (weaker)?
- Who funded it? (Industry-funded studies are not automatically wrong but warrant scrutiny.)
- Are the results being represented accurately in popular reporting?

Google Scholar (scholar.google.com) lets you find original studies for free. You don't need to read everything — even checking the abstract beats reading just the news article.

### Lesson 5.5 — Evaluating AI as a source

AI systems like ChatGPT and Claude are useful but unreliable for facts. They:

- Make confident statements that are wrong (hallucinations)
- May reflect biases in their training data
- Have knowledge cutoff dates
- Can't always verify their own claims

How to use AI as a source well:
- Use it for brainstorming, not facts
- Cross-check anything important with traditional sources
- Ask AI to cite sources, then verify those sources exist
- Be especially skeptical of specific names, dates, statistics, and quotes

### Lesson 5.6 — Local context matters

Sources optimized for one country often miss context in another. A US health study might not apply to African contexts. African journalism might not be well-represented in global media.

For African contexts specifically:
- Local sources often have ground truth that global outlets miss
- Global sources may oversimplify or stereotype
- Cross-reference major claims about Africa with African journalists and experts
- AfricaCheck, Premium Times, The Continent, Daily Maverick, Mail & Guardian, Stears Business — these provide critical local context

### Exercise 5.1 — The source diet audit

List the 10 sources you get most of your news from. Score each on the factors in Lesson 5.2. Are you getting diverse, high-quality information? If not, what would you change?

### Exercise 5.2 — The expert check

Pick a famous "expert" you follow (on YouTube, social media, podcasts). Research their actual credentials, track record, and incentives. Is the trust you place in them justified?

### Exercise 5.3 — The AI fact-check

Ask any AI 5 specific factual questions about topics you actually know about. Compare its answers to what you know. Note how often it's confidently wrong about things in your area of expertise. Now scale that to topics you don't know.

## Module 6: Systems Thinking

### Lesson 6.1 — Why things rarely have simple causes

Most explanations in public discourse are too simple. "X happened because of Y." Real causes are usually webs of factors, feedback loops, and unintended consequences.

The fuel subsidy gets removed in Nigeria. Transport costs rise. Food costs rise (because transport). Lots of small businesses cut staff. Unemployed people put pressure on remaining workers. Politicians blame each other. Crime rises in some areas. New informal businesses emerge to fill gaps. And so on.

That's a system. Tugging one thread changes many others. Systems thinking is the skill of seeing those webs instead of single threads.

### Lesson 6.2 — Feedback loops

The two basic types:

**Reinforcing loops** — change produces more change in the same direction.

> A celebrity has fans → fame brings more attention → attention brings more fans → fame increases → and so on.

**Balancing loops** — change produces resistance, returning to equilibrium.

> A market gets crowded with sellers → prices drop → some sellers leave → prices stabilize.

When you see something growing or shrinking, ask: what's reinforcing this? What's balancing it?

### Lesson 6.3 — Unintended consequences

Every action has effects beyond what was intended. Often these matter more than the intended effect.

Classic example: The Indian government wanted to reduce cobra populations in colonial-era Delhi. They offered bounties for dead cobras. Locals started farming cobras for the bounty. The government discovered this and cancelled the program. People released their farmed cobras. Cobra population went up.

The intended effect (reduce cobras) was overwhelmed by unintended consequences (perverse incentive to breed them, then dump them).

When evaluating any policy, business decision, or social change, ask: what might the unintended consequences be?

### Lesson 6.4 — Second-order effects

First-order effect: what happens immediately.

Second-order effect: what happens because of what happened.

Third-order effect: what happens because of that.

Smart thinkers consider multiple orders.

> Build a new road → first-order: easier transport.
> Second-order: more businesses set up along the road.
> Third-order: nearby town empties as people move closer to the new road.
> Fourth-order: that empty town becomes a problem for the government.

In personal decisions too:
> I take this scholarship → first-order: free education.
> Second-order: I'm bound to that location for 4 years.
> Third-order: I miss opportunities elsewhere.
> Fourth-order: my professional network forms there.

Most decisions aren't just about first-order effects.

### Lesson 6.5 — The map is not the territory

Every model, every theory, every analysis is a simplification. The real world is always more complex than our description of it.

This doesn't mean models are useless. It means we should remember they're models.

When someone offers you a clean framework that "explains everything," be skeptical. The world doesn't usually work in clean frameworks.

### Lesson 6.6 — Looking for what's not visible

Strong systems thinking includes asking: what am I not seeing?

- Who's affected by this decision that I'm not considering?
- What downstream effects am I ignoring?
- What feedback loops might kick in over time?
- What's the long-term version of this short-term win?

This is the difference between thinking and just reacting.

### Lesson 6.7 — Applying systems thinking to your life

This isn't just for politics or business. Apply it to your own life:

- Your daily habits are a system. Changes compound over years.
- Your relationships are a system. How you treat people shapes how they treat you, which shapes how you treat them.
- Your finances are a system. Small leaks now become big problems later.
- Your education is a system. Each course connects to others, opens or closes future doors.

You're not isolated decisions. You're a system in motion. The earlier you think this way, the better the system you build.

### Exercise 6.1 — Map a system

Pick a real-world issue you care about (climate change, youth unemployment, social media, anything). Draw a diagram of the factors involved and how they connect. Use arrows to show what affects what.

### Exercise 6.2 — Predict second-order effects

For one decision you're considering (small or large), list:
- First-order effects you expect
- Three possible second-order effects
- One unintended consequence that might happen

### Exercise 6.3 — Your life as a system

Map the major systems in your life — habits, relationships, school, work, money. For each, identify one positive feedback loop and one balancing loop. What are you reinforcing? What's keeping you stuck?

## Module 7: Disagreeing Well and Changing Your Mind

### Lesson 7.1 — Why most disagreements fail

Most disagreements aren't about finding truth. They're about winning, protecting identity, or expressing emotion. That's why they rarely change anyone's mind.

If you want to actually think clearly, you have to:
- Care more about being right than seeming right
- Be willing to change your mind when evidence demands it
- Engage with the strongest version of opposing views, not the weakest

This is hard. Most people, including most adults, never get good at it.

### Lesson 7.2 — Steelmanning vs strawmanning

Strawmanning: attacking the weakest version of an opposing view. Easy and useless.

Steelmanning: engaging with the strongest version of an opposing view. Hard and productive.

When you encounter a view you disagree with, ask: what's the smartest possible version of this argument? What would a thoughtful person who held this view say?

If you can't argue the opposing position well, you don't really understand the issue. You're just reacting to a caricature.

### Lesson 7.3 — The principle of charity

Assume the person you disagree with is reasoning in good faith. Even if they're wrong, assume they have reasons that make sense to them.

This shifts the question from "why are they so stupid/evil?" to "what makes them see it this way?" The second question is productive. The first is just venting.

Sometimes the answer is "they have different information." Sometimes "they have different values." Sometimes "they have different experiences that shape their interpretation." Often the answer is more interesting than expected.

### Lesson 7.4 — How to disagree productively

When you disagree with someone:

1. **Make sure you understand their position.** Restate it back to them. "What I hear you saying is..." If they confirm, you can engage. If they correct you, listen and try again.

2. **Acknowledge what's valid in their view.** Almost every position has something to it.

3. **Then state your disagreement clearly.** Without attacking the person.

4. **Offer evidence, not just opinions.** "Here's why I see it differently..." with reasons.

5. **Be willing to be wrong.** If they say something that changes your mind, say so.

6. **Know when to end the conversation.** Some debates aren't worth having. Some people aren't reasoning in good faith. That's okay.

### Lesson 7.5 — The art of changing your mind

You should be changing your mind regularly. If your views from a year ago are identical to today's, you probably aren't learning.

Signs you should change your mind:
- New evidence that contradicts your view
- A stronger argument you hadn't considered
- Realizing you'd been wrong about a key assumption
- Discovering your sources were unreliable
- Hearing from someone with direct experience you lack

Changing your mind isn't weakness. It's growth. The people who never change their mind aren't strong — they're stuck.

### Lesson 7.6 — When you're the wrong one

Sometimes you'll be in an argument and realize, partway through, that you're actually wrong. Most people double down at this point. Don't.

What to do:

- Acknowledge it. "Actually, you're right about that. I hadn't thought about it that way."
- Don't make a big deal of it. No need for excessive self-flagellation.
- Update your view going forward.

This builds trust faster than any other behavior. The people who can say "I was wrong" gain credibility that the people who can't will never have.

### Lesson 7.7 — When to stop arguing

Some disagreements aren't worth having:

- The other person isn't reasoning in good faith
- Emotions are too high for productive thinking
- The topic isn't important enough
- You're not going to change anything by continuing
- You're enjoying being right more than seeking truth

Recognize these. Step back. You don't need to win every argument.

### Exercise 7.1 — Steelman an opposing view

Pick a political or social view you strongly disagree with. Write a one-page argument FOR that position, as if you actually held it. Make it as strong as you can. Notice what it teaches you.

### Exercise 7.2 — The "I was wrong" practice

This month, find at least one situation where you can say "I was wrong" out loud. Without qualifications. Notice how it feels and how others respond.

### Exercise 7.3 — Track your mind changes

Keep a list this year of every belief or view you've changed. By next year, you should have at least 5–10 entries if you're actually thinking.

## Module 8: Capstone — Your Thinking Toolkit

### The Capstone

Build a personal thinking toolkit you'll use for the rest of your life.

**Part 1: Your beliefs audit**

List 10 strong beliefs you hold. For each, write:
- Where did this belief come from?
- What's the evidence for it?
- What's the evidence against it?
- What would change my mind?
- How confident am I, really? (1–10)

This is your starting point. Most beliefs won't change. Some will. The point is examination.

**Part 2: Your decision template**

Build a template you'll use for major decisions:

```
DECISION: [what am I deciding?]

OPTIONS: [what are the actual options? at least 3]

FOR EACH OPTION:
- First-order effects
- Second-order effects
- What I'm assuming
- What could go wrong

KEY UNKNOWNS: [what do I need to find out?]

MY BIASES IN THIS SITUATION: [what's pulling me toward a wrong answer?]

DECISION: [what I'm choosing]

CHECK-IN DATE: [when will I review whether this is working?]
```

Use this for any decision that matters. School, work, money, relationships.

**Part 3: Your source list**

Build a curated list of 15–20 high-quality sources covering different domains:
- News (at least 3 different perspectives)
- Long-form analysis
- Specific topics you care about
- One or two challenging your views

This becomes your information diet. Update it yearly.

**Part 4: Your thinking practices**

Adopt 3 regular practices:
- Daily: 10 minutes of reflection or journaling
- Weekly: One hour reviewing decisions and beliefs
- Monthly: Read or watch something from a perspective you disagree with

### Deliverable

A document containing:

1. Your beliefs audit (10 examined beliefs)
2. Your decision template
3. Your source list
4. Your thinking practices commitment
5. A short essay (300 words): "What kind of thinker am I trying to become?"
