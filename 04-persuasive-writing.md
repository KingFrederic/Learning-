# Course 4: Persuasive Writing and Clear Communication

**For learners 13–19 · Self-paced · ~10 hours**

In a world where AI can generate endless content, the writers who win are the ones who think clearly and say things others can't. Anyone can produce words. Few can move people with them.

This course teaches you to write so people actually listen — for essays, social media, school applications, emails, business pitches, anything. Clear writing equals clear thinking equals power.

## Module 1: Why Writing Matters More Than Ever

### Lesson 1.1 — The paradox

We're drowning in text. AI generates millions of words per second. Most of it is forgettable mush. In this environment, clear writing isn't less valuable — it's *more* valuable. Because almost nobody can do it.

Consider: every job you'll ever apply to, every grant you'll ever pursue, every product you'll ever launch, every relationship you'll ever negotiate — text will be involved. The person who can write a 4-sentence email that gets a "yes" beats the person who writes a 14-paragraph email that gets ignored.

Writing isn't a school subject. It's an operating system for the rest of your life.

### Lesson 1.2 — Why most writing fails

Bad writing fails for the same handful of reasons, over and over:

1. **The writer didn't think before writing.** They started typing and hoped the point would emerge.
2. **They were trying to sound smart instead of being clear.** Long words, complex sentences, pompous phrasing.
3. **They didn't know who they were writing to.** Wrote to "everyone" — which means no one.
4. **They didn't edit.** First drafts are always bad. Always.
5. **They had nothing real to say.** Filled space with words instead of substance.

Fix these and you're already in the top 10% of writers.

### Lesson 1.3 — The writer's three questions

Before you write a single word, answer these three questions in your head:

1. **Who am I writing for?** Be specific. Not "people interested in business." Try "Nigerian teenagers thinking about starting their first side hustle."

2. **What do I want them to think, feel, or do after reading?** "Think — wow, I could actually start something this week." "Feel — less scared." "Do — open a Selar store today."

3. **What's the one thing I need to say?** If you can't compress your point into one sentence, you don't know your point yet.

These three questions take 30 seconds. They'll save you hours of revising and embarrassment.

### Exercise 1.1 — The bad-writing audit

Find three things you've written in the last month (school essay, WhatsApp message thread, social caption). Identify which of the five failure modes each one fell into. Don't be gentle.

### Exercise 1.2 — The three-question warmup

For everything you write this week — every essay paragraph, every Instagram caption, every email — answer the three questions before writing. Just in your head. Notice if your writing gets sharper.

## Module 2: The Clear Sentence

### Lesson 2.1 — The principle: one idea per sentence

The number one rule of clear writing: each sentence carries one main idea. When you stuff three ideas into one sentence, readers get lost.

**Stuffed:**
> Although I had been planning to study abroad for years, I decided to stay in Nigeria for university because the tuition costs were lower, my family wanted me nearby, and I realized I could still build international opportunities through online networks.

**Unstuffed:**
> I had been planning to study abroad for years. I changed my mind. Three reasons: tuition was cheaper at home, my family wanted me close, and I realized I could build international networks online anyway.

The second version takes the same number of words but reads at twice the speed.

### Lesson 2.2 — Cut every word that doesn't earn its place

This is the hardest writing habit to build. Most sentences have 20–40% fat you can remove without losing meaning.

**Bloated:**
> In order to be able to successfully complete the project on time, it is necessary that we should consider the various factors involved.

**Trimmed:**
> To finish the project on time, we need to consider what could go wrong.

Words to cut on first pass: "very," "really," "quite," "actually," "basically," "literally," "just," "essentially," "in order to," "the fact that," "it is important to note that."

### Lesson 2.3 — Use specific over abstract

Abstract words make readers' eyes glaze over. Specific words paint pictures.

**Abstract:**
> The community faces many challenges that affect its development.

**Specific:**
> Kids in Makoko skip school when it rains because the wooden walkways flood. That's six lost days a month.

Same point. Different impact. Specificity is how you make people actually feel what you're saying.

### Lesson 2.4 — Active beats passive

**Passive:**
> Mistakes were made by the team.

**Active:**
> The team made mistakes.

**Passive:**
> The lesson was understood by the students.

**Active:**
> The students understood the lesson.

Active sentences have someone *doing* something. Passive sentences hide who did it. Use passive only when you genuinely don't know or care who did it ("the bridge was built in 1923"). Otherwise, active.

### Lesson 2.5 — Vary your sentence length

Three long sentences in a row exhaust readers. Three short sentences in a row sound like a robot.

**Monotonous:**
> I went to the market. I bought tomatoes. I came back home. I started cooking.

**Mixed:**
> I went to the market and bought tomatoes. Then I came home and started cooking — but the gas had finished.

Rhythm matters. Read your writing out loud. If it sounds like a drum solo, vary it.

### Exercise 2.1 — Sentence surgery

Take any 5-sentence paragraph you've written. Rewrite each sentence by:
1. Cutting at least 3 words
2. Making any passive sentences active
3. Replacing one abstract word with something specific

Compare before and after.

### Exercise 2.2 — Twitter discipline

Take a complex idea (something from one of your school subjects). Try to explain it clearly in 280 characters or less. Then try 140. Then 50. Each round forces sharper thinking.

### Exercise 2.3 — The boring sentence challenge

Write 10 sentences about your day, each starting with "I." Then rewrite the same 10 sentences without any of them starting with "I." Notice how much more interesting your writing gets when you vary the structure.

## Module 3: The Tight Paragraph

### Lesson 3.1 — What a paragraph is for

A paragraph delivers one complete idea. Not half an idea. Not three ideas. One.

When you change ideas, change paragraphs. Even if it's a short one. White space on the page tells the reader "we're moving to something new."

### Lesson 3.2 — The topic sentence

The first sentence of a paragraph should announce what the paragraph is about. Like a headline. The rest of the paragraph delivers on that promise.

**Bad paragraph:**
> Many things changed during my second year of secondary school. The food in the cafeteria got slightly better. Also, I learned a lot about myself. My grades improved too. Friends came and went.

That paragraph is about everything and nothing.

**Better:**
> My second year of secondary school was the year I learned to fail well. My math grade dropped in the first term. Instead of hiding it, I asked my teacher for help. By the third term, I was tutoring two classmates in the same subject I'd failed.

That paragraph has one idea (learning to fail well) and supports it with evidence.

### Lesson 3.3 — Show, don't just tell

This rule is repeated so often it becomes meaningless. Here's what it actually means:

**Telling:**
> My grandmother was a strict woman.

**Showing:**
> If I came home with my uniform untucked, my grandmother would meet me at the door and not say a single word. She'd just stare at the untucked shirt until I fixed it myself.

Telling delivers information. Showing delivers experience. Always reach for showing first.

### Lesson 3.4 — One idea, multiple angles

A good paragraph develops its single idea through multiple angles:

1. State the idea
2. Give an example
3. Explore an implication or twist
4. Land it

**Example:**
> Side hustles taught me more business than business class. (state)
> When I sold phone cases at school, I had to figure out pricing within a week, because if I charged too much nobody bought, and if I charged too little I lost money on shipping. (example)
> No textbook makes you feel that math the way an empty wallet does. (implication)
> Now in real economics class, I understand supply and demand because I lived it before I named it. (landing)

### Exercise 3.1 — The paragraph rebuild

Find one paragraph from a school essay you've written. Rebuild it using the structure above: topic sentence, example, implication, landing.

### Exercise 3.2 — Show, don't tell drill

Take these telling sentences and rewrite each as a showing scene (2–3 sentences):

1. The party was boring.
2. My uncle is generous.
3. The teacher was angry.
4. Lagos traffic is terrible.
5. She was nervous about the speech.

### Exercise 3.3 — One paragraph, three versions

Write the same paragraph three ways:
1. As if it's part of a serious essay
2. As if it's a friend texting you the same point
3. As if it's a tweet thread

Notice how voice changes by context. Same idea, different clothes.

## Module 4: Structure — How to Organize Anything

### Lesson 4.1 — Why structure matters

Readers can only hold so much in their head at once. Structure tells them where they are, where they're going, and why each part matters.

Bad structure = good ideas lost in the noise. Good structure = simple ideas land like punches.

### Lesson 4.2 — The classic essay structure (and why it still works)

You probably learned this in school. It actually works:

1. **Introduction** — what you're going to say
2. **Body paragraphs** — say it, with evidence
3. **Conclusion** — what you said and why it matters

The cliché version is boring because students treat it as a formula. Don't. Treat it as a frame, then make each section actually interesting.

### Lesson 4.3 — The question-driven structure

For more interesting essays, structure around a question:

1. **Pose a real question** — one you genuinely want answered
2. **Show why it matters**
3. **Explore the possible answers**
4. **Land on your answer** — even if it's "I don't know, but here's how I'd think about it"

This works for everything from school essays to blog posts to LinkedIn pieces.

### Lesson 4.4 — The story structure

Stories follow this shape:

1. **Setup** — who, where, when
2. **Conflict** — what went wrong or what's at stake
3. **Escalation** — it gets worse or harder
4. **Climax** — the moment of truth
5. **Resolution** — what happened
6. **Reflection** — what it meant

Use this for personal essays, applications ("tell us about a challenge"), and any time you're sharing experience.

### Lesson 4.5 — The PEEL paragraph (for arguments)

When making an argument, each paragraph should:

- **P**oint — make a claim
- **E**vidence — back it up
- **E**xplain — show why the evidence supports the claim
- **L**ink — connect to the next paragraph or back to the main argument

### Lesson 4.6 — Outlines save lives

For anything longer than 300 words, outline before writing. An outline is just a list of what each section/paragraph will be about. 2 minutes of outlining saves 30 minutes of rewriting.

**Sample outline for a 600-word personal essay:**
- Hook: the time I almost gave up on speaking French
- Setup: I had been studying French for 3 years, mostly in classroom
- Conflict: first conversation with a French native speaker — total disaster
- Insight: I'd been learning the wrong thing
- New approach: switched to consuming French media, talking to anyone
- Result: 6 months later, comfortable enough to do an interview in French
- Closing thought: the gap between knowing and doing

That outline took 90 seconds. Now writing the essay is just filling in the bullets.

### Exercise 4.1 — Outline first

Write outlines for 3 different essays you might write:
1. Why your favorite subject matters
2. A challenge you've overcome
3. Your opinion on a controversial topic

Don't write the essays. Just outlines. Get good at this before producing words.

### Exercise 4.2 — Restructure an old essay

Take an essay you wrote in school. Make an outline of what it actually said. Look at the outline — is it logical? Could it be reorganized to flow better? Reorganize, then rewrite.

### Exercise 4.3 — The 6-word story

Tell a complete story in 6 words. Yes, 6. Famous example: "For sale: baby shoes, never worn." Try 5 of your own. This is the ultimate exercise in structural compression.

## Module 5: Persuasion Without Manipulation

### Lesson 5.1 — The line between persuading and manipulating

**Persuading** = giving someone good reasons to agree with you, transparently.

**Manipulating** = tricking someone into agreeing by exploiting emotions, ignorance, or vulnerabilities.

Persuasion builds trust over time. Manipulation burns it. We're learning persuasion.

### Lesson 5.2 — The three ancient pillars (Aristotle was right)

For 2,400 years, the framework hasn't changed:

1. **Ethos** — credibility. Why should anyone listen to you?
2. **Pathos** — emotion. What do they feel when they read this?
3. **Logos** — logic. Does the argument make sense?

Strong writing uses all three. Weak writing relies on only one.

### Lesson 5.3 — Building ethos (credibility)

Ways to earn the right to be heard:

- Show you understand the other side
- Acknowledge what's complicated
- Reference your own experience honestly
- Cite real, verifiable sources
- Don't oversell

**Low ethos:**
> Everyone knows that social media is destroying young people's mental health.

**High ethos:**
> I deleted Instagram for 90 days last year. My anxiety dropped noticeably in the second week. I'm not the only one — a 2023 study in the Journal of Adolescent Health found similar effects, though the researchers warned about overstating causation.

### Lesson 5.4 — Using pathos (emotion) honestly

Emotion isn't manipulation when it's true. The trick: don't tell the reader what to feel. Show them something that *makes* them feel.

**Telling them how to feel:**
> It's heartbreaking that so many children in Lagos can't afford school supplies.

**Letting them feel it:**
> The boy at the bus stop sells groundnuts during school hours. He's twelve. He told me he stopped going to school last year because his mother couldn't afford the ₦200 weekly fee.

Emotion arrives uninvited when you give people specific human details. It never arrives when you announce it.

### Lesson 5.5 — Constructing logos (logic)

A logical argument has clear:
- **Claims** — what you're saying is true
- **Evidence** — why anyone should believe you
- **Reasoning** — how the evidence supports the claim

Watch out for these logical mistakes:

- **Strawman** — misrepresenting the other side's argument so it's easier to defeat
- **Ad hominem** — attacking the person instead of the argument
- **False cause** — assuming because A happened before B, A caused B
- **Bandwagon** — "everyone agrees" isn't an argument
- **Slippery slope** — assuming one step leads inevitably to disaster

### Lesson 5.6 — The reverse outline

When you're trying to be persuasive, outline what your readers will believe after each paragraph. If after paragraph 3 they should believe "this problem affects me personally," your paragraph 3 better make them feel that. Map the journey of conviction.

### Exercise 5.1 — The opposing view essay

Pick a position you disagree with. Write a 400-word essay arguing for it as persuasively as you can. This builds two skills: understanding opposing views, and recognizing how persuasion works on you.

### Exercise 5.2 — The hook test

Write 5 different opening sentences for an essay on the same topic. Read them out loud. Which one makes you actually want to keep reading? That's your hook. Use it.

### Exercise 5.3 — Find the manipulation

Look at 3 social media ads aimed at young people. Identify the persuasion tactics used (emotion, social proof, scarcity, etc.). Are they ethical persuasion or manipulation? Where's the line?

## Module 6: Writing for the Platform

### Lesson 6.1 — Format dictates voice

Same person, different platforms = different voice. A WhatsApp message to your best friend doesn't read like a scholarship application. Both can be authentic. They're just different contexts.

### Lesson 6.2 — School essays

The unwritten rules:

- Most teachers reward clear structure, not creative risks
- Hooks that surprise (a question, an unexpected statement, a scene) beat hooks that announce ("This essay will examine...")
- Specific examples beat general claims
- Don't quote dictionary definitions in your intro. Ever.
- Keep your conclusion short. Don't restate everything. End with something the reader will think about after closing the page.

### Lesson 6.3 — Personal statements and applications

The biggest mistake: trying to impress instead of trying to be real.

Universities and scholarship committees read thousands of applications. They can smell pretense from page one. What they want is a specific, honest, well-written story about who you actually are.

Tips:
- Start with a scene, not a thesis
- Pick one or two themes. Don't try to cover everything you've ever done.
- Show growth or change
- Be honest about struggle. Perfect candidates are boring candidates.
- The last paragraph should look forward, not just summarize

### Lesson 6.4 — Social media writing

The rules for high-engagement social posts:

**Twitter/X:**
- First line is everything. If they don't stop scrolling, the rest doesn't matter.
- One idea per post. Don't bury the point.
- Write like you talk
- Threads work when each tweet earns a continued read

**LinkedIn:**
- Personal stories beat corporate language
- Lessons from real experience beat thought-leader platitudes
- Short paragraphs (1–3 sentences) with line breaks
- End with a question to drive replies

**Instagram captions:**
- The first line shows in the feed — make it grab
- Use line breaks generously, walls of text get skipped
- A clear story or insight beats clever wordplay

**TikTok descriptions:**
- They're search bait. Use the words people would search for.
- Match the energy of your video

### Lesson 6.5 — Email writing

Most emails are too long, too vague, or too late. Fix all three:

**Bad email:**
> Hi Mrs. Adeyemi, I hope this email finds you well. I just wanted to reach out and let you know that I was thinking about the assignment that you gave us last week and I had some questions about it that I was hoping I could ask you, if that's okay. Specifically, I was wondering...

**Good email:**
> Hi Mrs. Adeyemi,
> 
> Quick question about last week's assignment: should the case study include data from before 2020, or only recent years?
> 
> Thanks,
> Adaeze

The good email respects the reader's time. The bad email steals it.

**Rules for emails:**
- Subject line that names the actual purpose
- Get to the point in the first sentence
- One topic per email
- Short paragraphs
- Clear ask or no ask, but don't bury what you need

### Lesson 6.6 — Business pitches and proposals

When you're trying to get someone to give you money, time, or attention:

1. **What's the problem?** (in their language, not yours)
2. **What's your solution?** (in one sentence)
3. **Why you?** (what makes you specifically able to do this)
4. **What do you need?** (specific ask)
5. **What's next?** (clear action step)

This works for sponsorships, business proposals, even asking your dad for a raise in your allowance.

### Exercise 6.1 — The same idea, four ways

Pick one idea you care about. Write about it as:
1. A Twitter thread (5 tweets)
2. A LinkedIn post (200 words)
3. An Instagram caption (100 words)
4. A short essay (500 words)

Notice how the writing transforms while the idea stays the same.

### Exercise 6.2 — Email rescue

Find the last long email you sent (to anyone). Rewrite it in 1/4 the length without losing the essential message.

### Exercise 6.3 — The hook collection

Save 20 social media posts or article openings that made you actually keep reading. Analyze what they have in common. This is research for your own writing.

## Module 7: Editing — The Real Skill

### Lesson 7.1 — Writing happens in editing

First drafts are always bad. Always. Even from professional writers. The actual skill of writing is *editing*. Cutting, rearranging, sharpening, finding the better word.

If you only write first drafts, you'll always sound like an amateur. Even spending 10 extra minutes editing transforms most writing.

### Lesson 7.2 — The cooling-off period

After you write something, walk away. Even for 30 minutes. You can't see your own writing clearly while it's still warm. The brain fills in what was *supposed* to be there.

For important pieces (applications, big essays), let it sit overnight before final edit.

### Lesson 7.3 — The reading-aloud test

Read your writing out loud. Where you stumble, the reader will stumble. Where you run out of breath, the sentence is too long. Where it sounds like a robot, it reads like a robot.

This is the single most underused editing technique. Use it.

### Lesson 7.4 — The cut-25% rule

After your first draft, force yourself to cut 25% of the words. You will think this is impossible. You will be wrong. The piece will be better.

This is the technique that produces tight, punchy writing.

### Lesson 7.5 — Levels of edit

When editing, work in passes. Don't try to fix everything at once.

1. **Structural edit** — does the order make sense? Are there sections to cut entirely?
2. **Paragraph edit** — does each paragraph have one clear idea? Do they flow?
3. **Sentence edit** — clear, short, varied? Active voice?
4. **Word edit** — better word choices? Cut filler?
5. **Proofread** — typos, punctuation, grammar

Each pass focuses on one level. Trying to do all five at once = doing none of them well.

### Lesson 7.6 — Using AI to edit (carefully)

AI is great at:
- Catching typos
- Suggesting alternative phrasings
- Pointing out unclear sentences
- Tightening verbose paragraphs

AI is bad at:
- Preserving your voice (it tends to flatten everything to generic professional)
- Knowing what's important to keep
- Adding actual insight

Use AI to suggest, not to rewrite. Always edit AI's edits to sound like you again.

### Exercise 7.1 — The 25% cut

Take any piece of writing 500+ words you've done. Cut 25% of the words. Compare. Notice it's better.

### Exercise 7.2 — Reading aloud

Take your most recent essay. Read it out loud, recording yourself. Listen back. Note every place you stumbled, sped up, slowed down, or sounded weird. Edit those spots.

### Exercise 7.3 — The five-pass edit

Take a 500-word piece. Edit it five times, each pass focused on one level (structure, paragraph, sentence, word, proofread). Compare your draft 1 to your draft 6.

## Module 8: Voice and Capstone

### Lesson 8.1 — What voice is

**Voice** is what makes your writing sound like *you* and not anyone else. Same facts, same arguments, but readers can tell who wrote it after three sentences.

Voice comes from:
- Word choices (formal vs casual, simple vs complex, regional flavors)
- Sentence rhythms (short choppy vs long flowing vs mixed)
- What you notice and what you ignore
- Your relationship to the reader (mentor, peer, observer, critic)
- Your sense of humor (or lack of it)
- The specific examples and references you reach for

You don't invent your voice. You uncover it by writing enough that the patterns emerge.

### Lesson 8.2 — How to find your voice

1. **Write a lot.** No shortcut here.
2. **Notice when you sound like you.** Some pieces feel right. Read them. Identify what makes them yours.
3. **Read writers you love.** Notice what they do. Steal techniques shamelessly. Eventually you'll fuse influences into something new.
4. **Stop trying to sound smart.** Trying-to-sound-smart kills voice instantly. Try to sound clear instead. Voice emerges from there.

### Lesson 8.3 — Voice and audience

Your voice doesn't change at its core, but it adapts to context. A great writer sounds recognizably like themselves whether writing a business email or a personal essay — but they don't write the email the same way as the essay.

Think of voice like having an accent. Your accent doesn't change when you talk to your grandmother versus your friend. But your vocabulary, formality, and tone do.

### Lesson 8.4 — Reading like a writer

The single best thing you can do to become a better writer: read more, and read like a writer.

Reading like a writer means: when you encounter a great sentence, stop and figure out *why* it works. When a paragraph moves you, ask what the writer did. Steal techniques. Study craft.

Most writers fail because they stopped reading.

### Lesson 8.5 — Building a writing practice

Writers write. That's the difference between a writer and someone who wants to be a writer.

Practical advice:
- Write something almost every day, even 200 words
- Keep a notes app for ideas
- Publish your writing somewhere — even just Substack or a Notion blog
- Share with friends and ask for feedback
- Track which pieces resonated most

The volume creates the skill. The publishing creates the accountability. The feedback creates the growth.

### Capstone Project — Your Writing Portfolio

Over 4 weeks, produce these four pieces:

**Week 1: Personal essay (800 words)**
Tell a true story from your life that taught you something. Use the story structure. Focus on showing, not telling.

**Week 2: Argumentative essay (600 words)**
Pick a real opinion you hold about something important. Argue it well, considering counterpoints.

**Week 3: Social media set**
Take the personal essay from week 1 and adapt it as:
- A LinkedIn post (200 words)
- A Twitter thread (5–8 tweets)
- An Instagram caption with 5 carousel slides outlined

**Week 4: Pitch or application**
Write either:
- An application essay (university, scholarship, internship)
- A pitch (sponsorship, partnership, business idea)
- A cover letter for a job you'd actually apply to

**Deliverable:** Put all four in a Google Doc or Notion page titled "My Writing Portfolio — 2026." This is the start of a real writing presence. Add to it for the rest of your life.
