# Course 9: Mental Health, Sleep, and Attention Hygiene

**For learners 13–19 · Self-paced · ongoing practice**

Nobody told us that mental health, sleep, and attention are skills you can build. We treated them like weather — happens to you, nothing to do about it. They're not weather. They're more like fitness. Trainable. Improvable. Worth working on every week of your life.

This course gives you the actual tools. Not motivational posters. Not "just think positive." The science-backed stuff that helps real teens function better, sleep better, focus better, and get help when they need it.

## Module 1: The Teen Brain Explained

### Lesson 1.1 — Why being a teen is genuinely harder

Your brain right now is doing more construction than at any time since you were three years old. Two specific things matter:

**The prefrontal cortex** — the part responsible for planning, impulse control, weighing consequences — doesn't fully develop until roughly age 25. Yours is still being built.

**The limbic system** — the emotional, reward-seeking part — is already on full power.

So you have an adult emotional engine and an apprentice driver. This isn't a flaw. It's normal teen neurology. It explains why emotions feel huge, why decisions feel urgent, why impulses are harder to resist.

Knowing this changes the question from "what's wrong with me?" to "what's normal for this stage, and how do I work with it?"

### Lesson 1.2 — The hormone storm

Puberty floods your brain with hormones that didn't exist at this level before. Mood swings, intense feelings, sudden energy crashes — all real, all biological.

This isn't an excuse. It's information. Your emotions are giving you bad data sometimes. Recognizing that is the first step to handling it well.

### Lesson 1.3 — Why your friends matter more than ever

Brain research shows the social part of teens' brains is hyperactive in a specific way. Belonging feels life-or-death. Social rejection literally activates pain centers.

This is evolutionary. Teens used to leave family groups to find new tribes, and getting accepted was actually survival. Your brain hasn't caught up to the fact that we live differently now.

What this means: your reactions to social situations are stronger than the situations objectively warrant. That's not weakness. It's biology. Knowing it helps you not react to every social drama like it's actually existential.

### Lesson 1.4 — What's normal vs what's a problem

These are normal teen experiences:

- Strong emotions that change quickly
- Caring deeply about peer opinions
- Feeling misunderstood by adults
- Periods of sadness, anxiety, or anger
- Self-doubt about appearance, identity, future
- Sleep schedule shifting later naturally

These suggest you might need support:

- Sadness that won't lift for weeks
- Loss of interest in everything you used to enjoy
- Changes in eating or sleeping that persist
- Constant worry that interferes with daily life
- Feeling worthless, hopeless, or numb most days
- Thoughts of hurting yourself

The first list — manage with the tools in this course. The second list — talk to a trusted adult and consider professional support. We'll cover how in Module 7.

### Exercise 1.1 — Brain education

Watch a short video on adolescent brain development (search YouTube: "adolescent brain development explained"). Write a paragraph on something you learned that surprised you about your own brain.

### Exercise 1.2 — Emotion tracking

For 7 days, rate your mood 1–10 three times daily (morning, afternoon, night). Note any major emotional moments. At the end of the week, look for patterns. Are some times of day reliably harder? After what triggers? This is data about you.

## Module 2: Sleep — Your Superpower

### Lesson 2.1 — Why teens need more sleep than they get

Most teens need 8–10 hours of sleep per night. Most teens get 5–7. This isn't laziness — it's a setup for damage.

Sleep is when your brain:
- Consolidates memory (everything you learned that day)
- Cleans out cellular waste (literally, via the glymphatic system)
- Regulates hormones
- Repairs muscle and tissue
- Processes emotions

Skipping sleep is skipping all of this. The effects compound. A few weeks of poor sleep impairs your learning, mood, immune system, and even your appearance.

### Lesson 2.2 — The teen sleep shift

Your circadian rhythm — the internal clock that tells you when to sleep — actually shifts later during teen years. So feeling sleepy at 11 PM instead of 9 PM is biology, not bad behavior.

But schools start early anyway. This mismatch means most teens are sleep-deprived even on a "normal" schedule. Working with your biology, not against it, helps.

### Lesson 2.3 — Sleep hygiene basics

The science of sleeping well is called **sleep hygiene**. The fundamentals:

**Consistent schedule.** Same bedtime and wake time, even on weekends (within an hour). Your body craves consistency.

**Dark room.** Even small amounts of light disrupt sleep. Use blackout curtains or a sleep mask. Cover blinking electronics.

**Cool room.** 16–20°C (60–68°F) is ideal. Lagos hot nights — use a fan or AC where possible.

**Quiet or consistent noise.** Total silence or steady white noise. Intermittent sounds wake you up.

**Phone away from bed.** This is the big one. Phone next to your head wrecks sleep multiple ways.

### Lesson 2.4 — Why phones destroy sleep

Three reasons:

**Blue light** suppresses melatonin (the sleep hormone). Phones in your face at night signal your brain that it's daytime.

**Dopamine hits** from scrolling keep you alert. Even when you put it down, your brain is buzzing.

**Notifications and access** mean you check "just one more thing" for hours.

The fix: phone outside the bedroom, or in airplane mode and across the room, starting an hour before bed. This single change improves most teens' sleep more than anything else.

### Lesson 2.5 — Wind-down routine

Build a 30–60 minute pre-sleep routine. Same things every night. Your brain learns these signals.

Examples:
- Shower
- Dim lights
- Read a physical book (not phone)
- Journal for 5 minutes
- Stretch
- Slow breathing

What NOT to do an hour before bed:
- Scroll social media
- Watch intense content
- Have heavy meals
- Drink caffeine (avoid after 2 PM)
- Have arguments via text
- Solve emotional problems

### Lesson 2.6 — When you can't sleep

If you've been in bed 20 minutes and can't sleep:

1. Get up
2. Go to another room (or chair)
3. Do something boring in dim light (read a chapter, fold laundry)
4. Return to bed when sleepy

Lying in bed frustrated trains your brain to associate bed with stress. Get up, reset, return.

If you're waking up at 3 AM with racing thoughts: keep a notebook by the bed. Write down what's bothering you, even just one sentence. Then deliberately switch focus to something neutral. Your brain offloads the thought.

### Lesson 2.7 — Caffeine and energy drinks

Caffeine has a half-life of 5–6 hours in most people. That means caffeine from your 4 PM coffee is still partially in your system at 10 PM.

Energy drinks are often worse — high caffeine, plus sugar, plus other stimulants. Some teens metabolize them poorly. They wreck sleep, anxiety, and heart rhythm.

Rule for teens: no caffeine after 2 PM. Avoid energy drinks entirely if you can. If you need to power through, water and a walk help almost as much.

### Exercise 2.1 — Sleep audit

For 7 days, track:
- What time you went to bed
- What time you fell asleep (approximately)
- What time you woke up
- How rested you felt (1–10)
- What you did in the hour before bed

At the end of the week, identify your worst night. What was different?

### Exercise 2.2 — The phone experiment

For 7 nights, put your phone in a different room (or far across yours) starting an hour before bedtime. Compare your sleep to the previous week. Most teens notice the difference within 3 days.

### Exercise 2.3 — Build your wind-down

Design your own 30-minute pre-sleep routine. Test it for 14 days. Adjust based on what works.

## Module 3: Attention and Focus

### Lesson 3.1 — Why focus feels broken

You're not imagining it. Your attention is harder to control than it would have been 20 years ago. Apps and platforms are built to capture and hold attention, designed by teams of psychologists and engineers. You against them isn't a fair fight.

This means your scattered attention isn't a personal failing. It's the predictable result of an environment designed to fragment focus. The good news: you can fight back with the right systems.

### Lesson 3.2 — The cost of switching

Every time you switch tasks, your brain pays a cost — sometimes 15+ minutes to fully re-engage. So 10 micro-distractions in an hour can leave you barely productive at all.

Multitasking is mostly a myth. You're not doing two things at once — you're switching rapidly and paying the cost each time.

The implication: real focused work happens when you stop switching. Long stretches of single-task time produce more in less time.

### Lesson 3.3 — Deep work vs shallow work

Computer scientist Cal Newport distinguishes:

**Deep work** — cognitively demanding tasks that benefit from prolonged focus. Studying hard material, writing, coding, creating.

**Shallow work** — easy tasks that can be done while distracted. Replying to messages, organizing files, scrolling.

Most teens do plenty of shallow work and almost no deep work. Then they wonder why they feel busy but unproductive.

You need both, but you have to schedule deep work deliberately. It won't happen by accident in a phone-saturated environment.

### Lesson 3.4 — How to build focus

**Single-task in blocks.** Pick one thing. Do only that for a set time (start with 25 minutes). No tabs, no phone, no music with lyrics.

**Pomodoro technique.** 25 minutes work, 5 minutes break. Repeat. After 4 rounds, take a longer break.

**Phone in another room.** Out of sight beats "in my pocket but silent." Distance is everything.

**Notifications off by default.** All apps. You check when you choose, not when they pull you. Whitelist only what truly needs interrupting.

**Browser tabs minimal.** If you have 30 tabs open, you have 30 partial attentions. Close most.

**Specific environment.** Same desk, same chair, same time of day. Brain learns "this is work mode."

### Lesson 3.5 — The two-screen problem

Studying with the TV on. Doing homework with Instagram open. "Background music" that's actually songs with lyrics.

These all kill focus, even when they feel fine. Research is clear: attention divided is attention destroyed.

If you need sound: instrumental music, lofi, brown noise, or silence. Lyrics compete with your language brain for processing.

### Lesson 3.6 — Social media attention drain

Social media isn't just time-wasting. It's attention-fragmenting. Even when you're not using it, knowing it's there pulls part of your mind.

Strategies that work:
- Specific times to check (not constantly)
- App timers (most phones have built-in tools)
- Logging out between sessions (friction matters)
- Removing apps from your phone, using only via browser
- One full day per week with no social media

You don't have to quit. Most teens can't anyway. But you can take back control.

### Lesson 3.7 — The boredom that builds focus

Modern teens almost never experience boredom. Phone in pocket, infinite content, never alone with your thoughts.

But boredom is where creativity comes from. Where insight happens. Where you get to know yourself.

Build small windows of intentional boredom:
- Walks without phones
- Sitting in waiting rooms without scrolling
- 10 minutes after waking, before checking anything

The discomfort is the work. It rebuilds your tolerance for not being entertained every second.

### Exercise 3.1 — The focus test

Sit down to do one piece of homework. Set a timer for 25 minutes. Phone in another room. Single tab. No music with lyrics. Just the task.

Most teens find this hard the first time. Notice how often your hand reaches for nothing. That's the habit you're breaking.

### Exercise 3.2 — Attention audit

Track how often you check your phone for one day. Use your phone's screen time feature to get an honest count. Most teens are shocked by their pickup numbers.

### Exercise 3.3 — One boring activity

Each day for a week, spend at least 15 minutes doing something without entertainment. Walk, sit, eat. No phone, no music, no media. Notice what happens in your head.

## Module 4: Stress and Anxiety Basics

### Lesson 4.1 — Stress isn't the enemy

A small amount of stress sharpens focus, motivates action, prepares you for performance. Athletes use it. Performers use it. You should too.

The problem isn't stress itself. It's:
- Stress that doesn't end
- Stress without recovery
- Stress about things you can't control

Distinguishing useful stress from harmful stress is half the battle.

### Lesson 4.2 — The body's stress response

When your brain detects threat (real or imagined), it triggers:
- Heart rate up
- Breathing faster, shallower
- Muscles tense
- Stomach upset
- Brain focused on threat, less on planning

This is your **fight-or-flight response**. Designed for short-term danger. Modern life triggers it for things it wasn't designed for — exams, social rejection, future uncertainty.

Knowing this is happening helps. It's a physical response, not just a mental one. You can address the physical side.

### Lesson 4.3 — Calming the body

When stressed, your body needs signals that it's safe. These work:

**Box breathing.** In for 4 seconds, hold 4, out 4, hold 4. Repeat 6 times. Calms your nervous system fast.

**Physiological sigh.** Two short inhales through the nose, one long exhale through the mouth. Even just one cycle helps. Research-backed and quick.

**Cold water on face/wrists.** Triggers the dive reflex, slows heart rate.

**Movement.** Walk, stretch, even shake out your arms. Stress is physical energy that needs to discharge.

**Grounding.** Look around. Name 5 things you can see, 4 you can hear, 3 you can touch, 2 you can smell, 1 you can taste. Pulls your brain back to the present.

### Lesson 4.4 — Anxiety vs healthy worry

**Healthy worry** — concern about something specific you can act on. Drives useful behavior.

**Anxiety** — worry that's persistent, exaggerated, often about things you can't control or that may never happen.

Anxiety becomes a problem when it:
- Persists for weeks
- Interferes with daily activities
- Causes physical symptoms (stomach issues, headaches, fatigue)
- Includes panic attacks
- Stops you from things you want to do

If that describes you, this is when professional help genuinely helps. We'll cover this in Module 7.

### Lesson 4.5 — Thoughts aren't facts

A core insight from cognitive behavioral therapy: your thoughts are not facts. They're your brain's interpretations. Often inaccurate.

When anxious, your brain offers thoughts like:
- "Everyone is judging me."
- "I'm going to fail."
- "Something terrible will happen."

These thoughts feel true. They're usually not. Test them:

- What's the evidence this is actually true?
- What would I say to a friend thinking this?
- What's the most likely outcome, not the worst case?
- Even if the worst happened, how would I handle it?

This isn't denial. It's accuracy.

### Lesson 4.6 — Reducing stress at the source

Some stress comes from real overload. The fix isn't coping — it's removing the cause.

Common teen overload sources:
- Overcommitment (too many activities, classes, responsibilities)
- Lack of downtime
- Toxic friendships or relationships
- Excessive academic pressure
- Sleep deprivation feeding everything else

Sometimes the answer isn't "manage the stress better." It's "reduce the load." Saying no, dropping activities, having honest conversations with parents or teachers.

### Exercise 4.1 — Stress mapping

List your top 5 sources of stress right now. For each, ask:
- Is this within my control?
- What can I actually do about it?
- Is there a way to reduce or eliminate this source?

Pick one to work on this month.

### Exercise 4.2 — Box breathing practice

Practice box breathing once a day, not just when stressed. 5 cycles, twice daily for 14 days. Train the response before you need it. When you actually need it, it'll come faster.

### Exercise 4.3 — Thought interrogation

When you catch an anxious thought today, write it down. Then answer:
- What's the evidence for this?
- What's the evidence against?
- What's a more accurate or balanced thought?

Do this for 3 thoughts. Notice how often the original thought was inflated.

## Module 5: Mood, Motivation, and the Bad Days

### Lesson 5.1 — Motivation is a myth (sort of)

The motivation people talk about — that feeling of energy and clarity that makes you want to work — comes after action, not before.

You don't usually feel motivated, then act. You usually act despite not feeling motivated, then the feeling follows.

This single insight changes everything. If you're waiting for motivation to show up, you're waiting forever. The trick is starting before you feel like it.

### Lesson 5.2 — The 5-minute rule

When you don't want to do something, commit to just 5 minutes. Not the whole task. Just start.

After 5 minutes, you'll usually want to continue. The friction of starting was the actual problem. Once started, momentum carries you.

If after 5 minutes you genuinely don't want to continue, you can stop. Permission to stop is what gets you started.

### Lesson 5.3 — Why bad days happen

Some days you feel low for no obvious reason. Brain chemistry, hormone cycles, weather, what you ate, how you slept, who you talked to. Sometimes you'll never know.

Bad days are normal. The mistake is treating each bad day as evidence of a broken self.

Rules for bad days:

1. **Lower the bar.** Don't try to be productive. Aim to not make things worse.
2. **Basic care.** Eat something. Drink water. Move at least a little.
3. **Reduce decisions.** Don't try to figure out your life today.
4. **Avoid social media.** It makes bad days worse.
5. **Talk to someone.** Even a 10-minute conversation helps.
6. **Trust it'll pass.** Bad days usually do.

### Lesson 5.4 — Mood-affecting basics

Things that affect mood more than people realize:

**Sleep.** Bad sleep guarantees bad mood. Always.

**Food.** Sugar crashes make you crash. Skipping meals tanks mood. Eat regularly.

**Movement.** Even 10 minutes of walking improves mood measurably. Not optional.

**Sunlight.** 10–15 minutes outside in morning sunlight sets your mood and sleep for the day.

**Social contact.** Even brief real-world conversations help. Texting alone often doesn't.

**Substance use.** Weed, alcohol, vaping — all affect teen brain development and mood. The trade-off is worse than the marketing suggests.

If your mood is consistently low, audit these basics first. Often they're the answer.

### Lesson 5.5 — Comparing yourself to others (the social media trap)

Social media is everyone's highlight reel compared to your behind-the-scenes. You see everyone at their best, edited and selected, while you live your messy real life.

This wrecks teen mood at scale. Research consistently shows heavy social media use correlates with anxiety, depression, and low self-esteem, especially in girls.

This doesn't mean quit social media. It means:

- Curate ruthlessly. Unfollow accounts that make you feel worse.
- Time-limit it.
- Remember that comparison kills joy.
- The people whose lives look perfect have problems you don't see.

### Lesson 5.6 — The negative self-talk loop

You talk to yourself all day in your head. Most teens talk to themselves more harshly than they'd ever talk to a friend.

"You're so stupid." "Nobody likes you." "You always mess up."

This kind of internal voice is a habit. You can change it. Not by forcing fake positivity, but by treating yourself with the basic kindness you'd extend to anyone else.

When you catch a harsh internal thought: ask, "Would I say this to my best friend?" If not, why are you saying it to yourself?

### Lesson 5.7 — Small wins compound

You don't fix your mood with one massive change. You fix it with many small things that add up.

Five minutes of sunlight. One glass of water. One text to a friend. Five push-ups. One paragraph of journaling. A real shower instead of doom-scrolling.

Stack small good things on bad days. Over time, the small things become how you live.

### Exercise 5.1 — The bad day protocol

Write your own "bad day protocol." A list of 5 small things you'll do when you're feeling low. Keep it on your phone. Use it next time.

### Exercise 5.2 — The 5-minute starter

This week, every time you don't want to do something necessary, commit to 5 minutes only. Track how often you continued past 5 minutes once you started.

### Exercise 5.3 — Inner voice audit

For one day, write down every harsh thing you say to yourself. At the end of the day, look at the list. Rewrite each one as you'd say it to a friend you cared about.

## Module 6: Building Daily Habits That Work

### Lesson 6.1 — Habits beat motivation

People who consistently take care of their health, mind, work — they're not more motivated than you. They've built systems that don't require motivation.

Brushing your teeth isn't motivating. You don't feel inspired to do it. You just do it because it's automatic.

The goal of habit-building is to make important things automatic so they happen without willpower.

### Lesson 6.2 — Habit stacking

The easiest way to build a new habit: attach it to something you already do.

**Formula:** "After [existing habit], I will [new habit]."

Examples:
- "After I pour my morning water, I'll do 10 push-ups."
- "After I sit down at my desk, I'll write tomorrow's top 3 priorities."
- "After I brush my teeth at night, I'll write 1 sentence in my journal."

Existing habits are reliable triggers. New habits stick better when anchored to them.

### Lesson 6.3 — Start tiny

Most teens fail at habits by trying to start too big. "I'll go to the gym for an hour every day." Lasts 4 days.

Start so small it's almost embarrassing:
- Do 1 push-up daily (not 50)
- Write 1 sentence in your journal (not a page)
- Read 1 page (not a chapter)
- Meditate for 1 minute (not 20)

Once the habit is built (showing up reliably for 30+ days), then increase.

### Lesson 6.4 — The 2-day rule

Never miss the same habit twice in a row.

Missing once is normal life. Missing twice is the start of quitting. So the rule is simple: it's okay to skip a day. It's not okay to skip two.

This single rule has saved more habits than any other.

### Lesson 6.5 — Environment design

The biggest predictor of habit success: your environment.

Want to read more? Phone in another room, book on your pillow.

Want to drink more water? Bottle on your desk where you see it constantly.

Want to study better? Set up a dedicated study space, even just a corner.

Want to scroll less? Phone in a different room during study, social media apps deleted from phone.

You're not stronger than your environment. Build environments that make good choices easy and bad choices hard.

### Lesson 6.6 — Tracking without obsession

Tracking habits creates accountability. But obsessive tracking can become its own stress.

Simple options:
- Calendar with X marks (Jerry Seinfeld's "don't break the chain")
- Habit tracker apps (Streaks, Habitica, Loop Habit Tracker — many free)
- Bullet journal layouts
- Just a Google Sheet

Pick one method. Use it. Don't track 40 habits at once — pick 1–3 to focus on for 30 days, then add more.

### Lesson 6.7 — The 8 habits worth building before 20

If you're going to invest in habits as a teen, these compound massively:

1. **Daily reading** — even 10 pages adds up
2. **Daily journaling** — even 3 sentences
3. **Daily movement** — walk, exercise, sport
4. **Adequate sleep** — same time most nights
5. **Daily water** — easy but most teens fail
6. **Weekly reflection** — review your week, plan the next
7. **Phone limits** — defined times for social media
8. **One creative practice** — writing, music, art, coding, anything

These don't make you cool. They make you formidable. By 25, you'll be unrecognizable from the version of you who didn't.

### Exercise 6.1 — Pick your starter habit

Choose ONE habit to build over the next 30 days. Pick from the 8 above or invent your own. Make it tiny (1-minute version).

### Exercise 6.2 — Habit stack design

Identify 3 existing habits in your day. For each one, design a small new habit to attach. Write the formula: "After [existing], I will [new]."

### Exercise 6.3 — Environment audit

Look at your bedroom and study area. Identify 3 changes that would make good habits easier. Make those changes today.

## Module 7: When to Ask for Help

### Lesson 7.1 — Asking for help is hard for teens

Many teens hesitate to ask for help with mental health because:

- They think they should handle it alone
- They fear judgment from family or friends
- They don't know what's "serious enough"
- They worry about being labeled or seen differently
- Cultural stigma — especially in some African families — treats mental health struggles as weakness

All these are understandable. None of them are good reasons to suffer alone when help exists.

### Lesson 7.2 — Signs you should reach out

You don't need to be in crisis to ask for help. Reach out if you experience:

- Persistent sadness or hopelessness for more than 2 weeks
- Loss of interest in things you usually enjoy
- Changes in eating or sleeping that won't resolve
- Trouble functioning at school or with friends
- Frequent intense anxiety or panic
- Feeling overwhelmed and unable to cope
- Self-harm or thoughts of self-harm
- Thoughts of suicide
- Trauma or abuse you're carrying alone

If you're not sure whether what you're feeling counts, that's already reason enough to talk to someone.

### Lesson 7.3 — Who to talk to first

Trusted adults in your life:

- A parent or guardian (if safe to talk to about this)
- An older sibling or cousin
- An uncle or aunt
- A teacher you trust
- A school counselor
- A pastor, imam, or spiritual leader
- A coach or mentor
- A friend's parent

Start with whoever feels safest. You don't have to have everything figured out before talking. Just start.

### Lesson 7.4 — Professional help

For deeper or persistent issues, professional mental health support is the right step.

**Therapists/counselors** are trained to help with mental health. Sessions are confidential (with limited exceptions for safety). Talking to a therapist isn't weakness — it's getting an expert on your team.

In many African contexts, finding affordable therapy can be hard. Some options:

- School counselors (often free for students)
- University clinics (sometimes serve community)
- Online therapy platforms (sometimes have sliding-scale fees)
- Community mental health programs
- Religious counseling (varies in quality, but can be a start)

For Nigerian teens specifically, some resources:
- Mentally Aware Nigeria Initiative (MANI)
- She Writes Woman Mental Health Initiative
- Mindcare Nigeria
- Asido Foundation

### Lesson 7.5 — What therapy actually is

Many teens have wrong ideas about what therapy is. It's not:

- Talking on a couch about your childhood for years
- A diagnosis of being "crazy"
- Being told what to do
- Embarrassing or shameful

It is:

- A trained professional helping you understand yourself and develop tools
- Confidential conversation in a safe space
- Practical, often skill-based
- Goal-oriented (you're working toward something)
- Worth trying even if you're not in crisis

### Lesson 7.6 — Helping a friend who's struggling

If a friend tells you they're struggling, you don't have to fix it. Your job is to listen, take it seriously, and help them get to better support.

What helps:

- Listen without judgment
- Take what they say seriously
- Don't try to immediately solve it
- Ask what they need
- Suggest talking to a trusted adult or professional
- Stay in touch — don't disappear

If a friend mentions self-harm or suicide:

- Take it seriously. Always.
- Don't promise to keep it secret
- Tell a trusted adult who can help
- Stay with them or stay in contact until help is available

Saving a friend's life is more important than keeping their secret. They may be angry at first. They will likely thank you eventually.

### Lesson 7.7 — Crisis resources

If you or someone you know is in immediate crisis (thoughts of suicide, severe self-harm, abuse):

**Nigeria:** Mentally Aware Nigeria Initiative — 0809 210 6493

**Kenya:** Befrienders Kenya — +254 722 178 177

**South Africa:** SADAG — 0800 567 567

**International:** International Association for Suicide Prevention has a directory of crisis lines worldwide: iasp.info/resources/Crisis_Centres

If someone is in immediate danger, treat it as an emergency.

### Exercise 7.1 — Build your support map

List 5 people you could turn to if you were struggling. Include at least one adult. Save their contact info somewhere accessible.

### Exercise 7.2 — Research local resources

Find at least 2 mental health resources in your country (not just hotlines — therapists, support groups, school counselors, online services). Save the contact info for future reference.

### Exercise 7.3 — Have one conversation

This month, have one honest conversation with someone you trust about how you're actually doing. Not the "fine" answer. The real one. This builds the muscle for when you really need it.

## Module 8: Capstone — Your Wellbeing Operating System

### The Capstone

Build a personal "wellbeing operating system" you'll actually use. Not a perfect plan. A real, sustainable one.

**Step 1: Audit (week 1)**

Honestly assess where you are on:
- Sleep (average hours, consistency)
- Stress (current level 1–10)
- Mood (recent average)
- Energy (mornings, afternoons)
- Connection (last meaningful conversation)
- Movement (typical week)
- Focus ability (1–10)
- Phone/social media (hours per day)

This is your baseline.

**Step 2: Foundation habits (weeks 2–4)**

Pick 3 habits to build:
- One for sleep
- One for attention/focus
- One for mood/movement

Make them tiny. Use habit stacking. Track simply.

**Step 3: Crisis plan (week 5)**

Write down:
- 5 people you can reach out to
- 5 things that consistently help your mood
- 3 places to go when overwhelmed
- 3 things you'll avoid when struggling
- Key crisis numbers in your country

Save this somewhere accessible. You probably won't need it. If you do, you'll be glad.

**Step 4: Maintenance (ongoing)**

- Weekly check-in: rate yourself on the audit categories, adjust as needed
- Monthly reflection: what's working, what isn't?
- Quarterly review: are you better or worse than 3 months ago?

### Deliverable

A document (Notion page, Google Doc, journal section) containing:

1. Your baseline audit
2. Your 3 foundation habits and tracking system
3. Your crisis plan
4. Your weekly check-in template
5. A statement to your future self about why this matters

### When to seek professional help

The honest answer: sooner than you think. Mental health is like physical health. You see a dentist for prevention, not just emergencies. The same applies here.

If you've read this whole course and felt like much of it described you — talk to someone. Not because anything is wrong with you. Because you deserve support that goes beyond a course.
