# Course 2: Financial Literacy & Personal Economics

**For learners 13–19 · Self-paced · ~12 hours**

Nobody teaches you this in school and that's not an accident. The kids who learn how money actually works before they're 20 spend their adult lives building wealth. The kids who don't spend their adult lives drowning in small decisions that compound badly.

This course is the one I wish someone had taught me at 14. By the end, you'll understand money, banking, investing, debt, and how to think about wealth in a way that'll change your life.

## Module 1: What Money Actually Is

### Lesson 1.1 — Money is a story we all agree to believe

Take a ₦1,000 note out of your pocket (or any banknote). What is it actually? Paper with ink. Worth less than ₦5 to produce. But you can trade it for goods worth ₦1,000 anywhere in Nigeria.

Why? Because everyone agrees it's worth that. That's it. That's the whole secret.

This isn't a small point. Money is a shared belief system. If everyone in Lagos woke up tomorrow and decided naira was worthless, it would be worthless. This happened in Zimbabwe in 2008. People used wheelbarrows to carry money to buy bread.

Understanding this changes how you see money. It's not a thing — it's a *relationship*. A measure of trust. When you "save money," you're really storing trust to use later.

### Lesson 1.2 — The four functions of money

Economists say money does four things:

1. **Medium of exchange** — you can trade it for stuff
2. **Store of value** — you can save it for later
3. **Unit of account** — it gives us a way to measure value (this shirt costs ₦5,000)
4. **Standard of deferred payment** — you can use it to settle debts

When a currency stops doing one of these, people stop using it. Naira is great for #1 and #3 in Nigeria, weaker for #2 because of inflation (we'll cover this later).

### Lesson 1.3 — Cash vs digital money vs crypto

You're growing up in the most interesting time for money in human history. Three systems exist at once:

**Cash** — physical bills and coins. Anonymous. Universal. Doesn't need internet. But heavy, losable, and limited.

**Digital money** — what's in your bank account, mobile money (M-Pesa, Opay, Kuda, Wave), card payments. This is most money now. Faster, traceable, needs infrastructure.

**Cryptocurrency** — Bitcoin, Ethereum, others. Digital money not controlled by any government. Volatile (price swings wildly). Useful for some things, dangerous for others. We won't tell you to buy or not buy crypto, but you should know it exists and roughly how it works.

### Exercise 1.1 — Track your money's flow

For one week, write down every single time money moves through your life. Lunch money, transport, gifts, anything. By the end of the week you'll see patterns you didn't know existed.

### Exercise 1.2 — Money as a story

In 200 words, write your own definition of what money means *to you*. Not what dictionaries say. What you think. Save this and re-read it in 6 months.

## Module 2: Budgeting That Doesn't Suck

### Lesson 2.1 — Why most budgets fail

Most people quit budgeting in 2 weeks. Here's why: they treat it like a diet. Restrictive, joyless, all about what you *can't* spend. That never lasts.

A good budget is the opposite: it's a plan that makes your money do what *you* actually want. If you love watching movies, your budget should include movies. If you love clothes, include clothes. The point isn't to suffer. The point is to spend on what matters and stop bleeding money on what doesn't.

### Lesson 2.2 — The 50/30/20 rule (adjusted for teens)

The classic adult rule: 50% needs, 30% wants, 20% savings.

For a teen with no rent or bills, flip it:

- **40% spending** (what you want now)
- **40% saving** (future you will thank you)
- **20% giving/sharing** (gifts, helping family, charity)

These percentages aren't sacred. The point is having *some* split, instead of spending everything that hits your hands.

### Lesson 2.3 — Track first, budget second

Don't make a budget yet. Track for 2 weeks first. Write down every naira/cedi/franc that comes in and goes out. Then look at the patterns.

You'll find things like: "I spent ₦3,200 on small chops last week." That's not a problem unless small chops aren't important to you. The data lets you decide what to keep and what to cut.

### Lesson 2.4 — The envelope method (physical or digital)

Old-school but works. You divide your money into envelopes for different purposes. When the envelope is empty, you're done spending in that category.

Modern version: separate sub-accounts in your bank app. Most Nigerian fintech apps (Kuda, Opay, PiggyVest) let you create "pockets" or "safelocks" for different goals.

Example for a 16-year-old with ₦10,000/month from parents or odd jobs:

- ₦4,000 spending money (food, transport, fun)
- ₦4,000 long-term savings (locked, don't touch)
- ₦1,000 gift fund (birthdays, helping)
- ₦1,000 emergency fund

### Lesson 2.5 — The 24-hour rule

Want to buy something over a certain amount (say ₦5,000)? Wait 24 hours. If you still want it tomorrow, buy it. You'll be shocked how many purchases die overnight.

For bigger purchases (₦20,000+), wait a week. For huge ones (₦100,000+), wait a month.

### Exercise 2.1 — The 14-day tracker

Track every naira (or your local currency) for 14 days. At the end, sort into categories. Identify your top 3 spending categories. Ask yourself: are these the things I actually value most? If not, you've found something to fix.

### Exercise 2.2 — Your first budget

Based on your 14-day tracker, build a monthly budget using the 40/40/20 framework. Write it down. Live by it for one month. Adjust at the end.

### Exercise 2.3 — The "no spend" challenge

Pick a non-essential category (snacks, online shopping, whatever). Don't spend on it for 14 days. Track how much you saved. Notice how it felt.

## Module 3: Saving and the Magic of Compound Interest

### Lesson 3.1 — Why your future self should rob your present self

If you save ₦1,000 a week from age 15 to age 65, and earn 10% interest yearly, you'll have roughly ₦60 million. ₦60 million from putting away ₦1,000 a week.

Most of that money isn't from your savings. It's from **compound interest** — interest earning interest earning interest. The money makes more money, which makes more money. Einstein supposedly called compound interest "the eighth wonder of the world." (He probably didn't, but it's a good quote.)

Here's the thing nobody tells you: the earlier you start, the less you have to save. Someone who starts at 15 and stops at 25 will have *more money at retirement* than someone who starts at 25 and saves until 65. Time matters more than amount.

### Lesson 3.2 — How compound interest actually works

Imagine you put ₦10,000 in an account earning 10% per year.

- End of year 1: ₦11,000 (you earned ₦1,000)
- End of year 2: ₦12,100 (you earned ₦1,100 — interest on interest)
- End of year 3: ₦13,310 (you earned ₦1,210)
- End of year 10: ₦25,937
- End of year 20: ₦67,275
- End of year 30: ₦174,494
- End of year 50: ₦1,173,909

You didn't add anything after the first ₦10,000. It just grew. That's compounding.

### Lesson 3.3 — Where to actually put your savings

Don't keep savings in your spending account. You'll spend them. Options for African teens:

**Savings apps with interest:**
- PiggyVest (Nigeria) — 10–15% annual interest, can lock funds
- Cowrywise (Nigeria) — similar, with investment options
- M-Pesa savings (Kenya, expanding)
- Wave (Senegal, Côte d'Ivoire) — basic savings

**Bank fixed deposits:** Lock money for 3, 6, or 12 months. Higher interest than regular savings.

**Digital dollar accounts:** Some platforms (like Risevest, Bamboo) let you save in dollars, which protects you from naira depreciation. Useful for long-term savings.

**Don't put long-term savings in:** crypto (too volatile for beginners), random "investment opportunities" your friends pitch you, anything promising "guaranteed 50% returns" (that's a scam, always).

### Lesson 3.4 — Emergency funds before everything else

Before investing, before saving for cool stuff, build an **emergency fund**: 1 month's worth of expenses you can access instantly. This is the money that saves you when something unexpected happens.

For a teen, this might be ₦20,000–₦50,000. For an adult, 3–6 months of expenses. Boring? Yes. Essential? Also yes.

### Exercise 3.1 — The compound interest calculator

Go to investor.gov/financial-tools-calculators/calculators/compound-interest-calculator (or just search "compound interest calculator").

Play with these scenarios:
- Save ₦500/week from age 15 to 65, 10% return
- Save ₦2,000/week from age 25 to 65, 10% return
- Save ₦10,000/week from age 40 to 65, 10% return

Notice which one ends up with the most money. Sit with that.

### Exercise 3.2 — Open your first savings account

If you don't have one: open a savings account this week. Most Nigerian neobanks let teens open accounts with a guardian. Goal: deposit ₦1,000 minimum (or your local equivalent), don't touch it for 30 days.

### Exercise 3.3 — Build your emergency fund

Calculate how much you'd need to cover one month of basic spending. Start saving toward that number. When you hit it, lock it somewhere annoying to access.

## Module 4: Investing Basics

### Lesson 4.1 — Saving vs investing

**Saving** = putting money somewhere safe where it doesn't grow much. Good for short-term goals and emergencies.

**Investing** = putting money somewhere it might grow a lot. Has risk. Good for long-term goals.

The longer your time horizon, the more you should invest vs save. If you need the money in 1 year, save it. If you don't need it for 10 years, invest it.

### Lesson 4.2 — The main things you can invest in

**Stocks (shares)** — You buy a tiny piece of a company. If the company does well, your piece is worth more. Companies you can buy: MTN, Dangote Cement, Access Bank in Nigeria. Apple, Google, Amazon in the US. You make money two ways: the share price goes up, or the company pays you part of its profits (called dividends).

**Bonds** — You lend money to a government or company. They pay you interest. Safer than stocks, lower returns.

**ETFs (Exchange-Traded Funds)** — A bundle of stocks or bonds. Instead of betting on one company, you bet on many at once. Way safer for beginners.

**Real estate** — Land or buildings. Traditional African store of wealth. Hard to start with small money, but apps now let you invest in real estate fractions.

**Mutual funds** — Like ETFs but managed by professionals. Slightly higher fees, slightly more guidance.

**Commodities** — Gold, silver, oil. Used by some as inflation protection.

**Crypto** — Bitcoin, Ethereum, others. Volatile. Risky. Treat as gambling money you can afford to lose, not investing money.

### Lesson 4.3 — The single most important investing rule

**Diversify.** Don't put all your money in one investment. If one fails, the others survive.

This is why ETFs are perfect for beginners. One ETF can hold hundreds of companies. If one goes bankrupt, your investment barely notices.

The classic beginner ETF in the US is something tracking the S&P 500 — basically the 500 biggest US companies. Historically averages about 10% per year over long periods. Boring. Effective.

### Lesson 4.4 — Investing for African teens

You have options. Here's the honest landscape:

**Local stock market (Nigerian Exchange):**
- Apps like Bamboo, Trove, Chaka let you buy Nigerian stocks
- Most banks also offer brokerage accounts
- You can start with ₦1,000

**Global stocks (US market) from Africa:**
- Bamboo, Trove, Risevest let Nigerians buy US stocks
- You're investing in dollars, which protects against naira depreciation
- Can buy fractional shares (a piece of Apple stock, not a whole share)

**Risevest:** Pre-built dollar investment plans. Good entry point.

**Cowrywise / PiggyVest investment options:** Curated funds with various risk levels.

### Lesson 4.5 — When NOT to invest

Don't invest:
- Money you'll need within 12 months (use savings)
- Money you can't afford to lose (use emergency fund)
- Borrowed money (you can lose double — the investment AND owe the debt)
- In things you don't understand (if your cousin pitches you on a "guaranteed crypto opportunity," walk away)

### Lesson 4.6 — Scams to know

If you take nothing else from this module, take this: **anyone promising you guaranteed high returns is scamming you.** Period. End.

Real investing earns roughly 7–12% per year on average, with risk of losses. Anyone offering 30%, 50%, "double your money in a month" is running a Ponzi scheme. People will swear it's real because they got paid early. The early people always get paid. The late people lose everything. MMM, Get Help Worldwide, every crypto "investment club" — same script.

Rule: if you can't explain *exactly* how the money is being made, don't put your money in.

### Exercise 4.1 — The fake portfolio

Pretend you have $1,000 (or ₦500,000). Pick 5 companies you understand and "buy" them in your notebook. Use the current stock price. Check the value every week for 3 months. See how your fake portfolio does. This teaches you investing without losing real money.

Companies to consider: Apple, Tesla, Amazon, MTN, Dangote, Coca-Cola, Netflix, anything you actually understand.

### Exercise 4.2 — Open an investment account

Once you're 18 (or with a guardian if younger): open a Bamboo, Trove, or Risevest account. Don't fund it yet. Just go through the process. Familiarize yourself.

### Exercise 4.3 — Research one investment

Pick one company or ETF. Spend 2 hours researching it:
- What do they do?
- How do they make money?
- Are they growing?
- What are their risks?

Write a 1-page summary. This is real research. Most adults don't do this before investing.

## Module 5: Debt — The Trap and the Tool

### Lesson 5.1 — All debt is not the same

Most people lump all debt together. Don't. There's a huge difference between:

**Productive debt** — borrowing that makes you richer over time:
- Education loans (sometimes)
- Mortgage on a house you live in
- Business loan for a profitable business

**Destructive debt** — borrowing that makes you poorer:
- Credit card debt (carrying balances)
- Buy-now-pay-later for things you don't need
- Loan apps with high interest (more on these)
- Borrowing to fund a lifestyle

### Lesson 5.2 — How interest rates destroy people

A ₦50,000 loan from one of those popular loan apps might charge "5% per week." Sounds small. Annually, that's 260%+ interest. Borrow ₦50,000, owe ₦180,000 by year-end if you don't pay it down. This is how people get destroyed.

The most dangerous loans in Africa right now: predatory mobile loan apps. They give instant cash, then trap people in debt spirals, then harass family members listed in your phone contacts.

If you ever feel desperate enough to consider one: don't. Borrow from family. Sell something. Find another way.

### Lesson 5.3 — Credit cards

Credit cards aren't evil. Used right, they're powerful: cashback, fraud protection, building credit history.

Used wrong, they're catastrophic. The trap: you only have to pay the "minimum payment" each month. Pay only the minimum and you'll be in debt for decades. The interest will outpace your payments.

**Credit card rules:**
1. Only spend what you can pay off in full each month
2. Pay the full balance, not the minimum, every single time
3. Use the card like a debit card — only spend what's actually in your account

### Lesson 5.4 — Buy Now Pay Later (BNPL)

Apps like Carbon, FairMoney, Klarna, Affirm let you buy something now and pay later. Sometimes useful (split a real expense into manageable chunks, no interest if you pay on time). Often dangerous (you buy stuff you can't actually afford because the monthly looks small).

Use BNPL only if:
1. You'd buy the item even if you had to pay in full
2. You're 100% sure you can make the payments
3. You're getting a clear benefit (0% interest, etc.)

### Lesson 5.5 — Student loans (for the older teens)

If you're considering university and might need loans: understand exactly what you're signing.

- Total amount you'll owe by graduation
- Interest rate (and when it starts accruing)
- Monthly payment after graduation
- How that compares to expected salary in your field

Don't take loans that'll require half your future salary to pay back. A useless degree paid for with debt is a 30-year financial sentence. A useful degree paid for cheap or free is the best investment you can make.

### Exercise 5.1 — Debt audit

If you have any debt right now (borrowed from a friend, family, app): list it all. Amount, interest rate, when it's due. Make a plan to pay it off. Highest interest rate first.

### Exercise 5.2 — The credit card simulation

Look up a credit card's minimum payment formula online. Calculate: if you have ₦100,000 in credit card debt at 30% annual interest, and only pay the minimum each month, how long until it's paid off and how much total interest you'll pay.

The answer will horrify you. That's the point.

### Exercise 5.3 — The loan app conversation

Talk to one adult you trust who has used a Nigerian loan app. Ask them honestly how it went. What did they borrow, what did they pay back, would they do it again? Real stories teach better than warnings.

## Module 6: Earning Your First Real Money

### Lesson 6.1 — Three ways to make money

There are exactly three ways to earn:

1. **Sell your time** — jobs, hourly work
2. **Sell a product** — physical or digital things you make/resell
3. **Sell a service** — your skills applied to someone's problem

Most adults only know #1. The wealthy mix all three. Start mixing early.

### Lesson 6.2 — Side hustles for teens (that actually work)

These work for teens with no capital and no experience:

**Tutoring** — younger kids in subjects you're good at. Start with cousins, expand by word of mouth. ₦2,000–₦5,000 per session is normal.

**Reselling** — buy in bulk (snacks, accessories, phone cases), sell at school or on WhatsApp. Margins of 30–50%.

**Content creation** — TikTok, YouTube Shorts, Instagram Reels. Takes time, but compounds. The earlier you start, the better.

**Freelance skills** — graphic design (Canva is free), social media management for small businesses, video editing (CapCut is free), writing.

**Digital products** — Notion templates, study guides, prompt packs (relevant after this course series), printables. Sell on Selar, Gumroad, Paystack.

**Local services** — washing cars, walking dogs, helping older neighbors with tech, photography for events.

### Lesson 6.3 — How to charge for your work

Most beginners undercharge wildly. Fix this:

**For services:** Look up what professionals charge. Charge 25–50% of that as a beginner. Raise prices as you get better and busier.

**For products:** Calculate cost to make + your time + profit margin. Most successful sellers aim for 2–3x their cost.

**Never:** charge so little you resent the work. Underpriced work breeds bad attitude, which loses customers.

**Always:** get paid upfront for new clients. Half upfront, half on delivery is also fine. Never deliver before payment for a first-time client.

### Lesson 6.4 — Building your first customer base

Your first 10 customers come from people who already know you. Period.

1. Tell every family member what you do
2. Post about it on your WhatsApp status weekly
3. Ask satisfied customers to refer one person
4. Deliver excellent work for the first 10 — they become your marketing

Don't waste money on Instagram ads until you've made your first ₦100,000 from referrals.

### Lesson 6.5 — Reinvesting your earnings

When you start making money, the temptation is to spend it all on stuff you couldn't afford before. Don't.

**The 50/30/20 for earned money:**
- 50% back into the business (better tools, more inventory, learning)
- 30% personal use (you earned it, enjoy some of it)
- 20% savings/investing

By year 2, your business should be funding itself and generating real profit.

### Exercise 6.1 — Skill inventory

List 10 things you're good at. Not "good for your age" — actually good. Then circle 3 that someone might pay you to do. Then pick 1 to start with.

### Exercise 6.2 — Your first sale

This month: make one actual sale of your skill or product. Charge real money. Even if it's ₦1,000. The first sale is the hardest psychological barrier. Get past it.

### Exercise 6.3 — Customer interview

Find one person who'd be your ideal customer. Talk to them (not selling, just listening). Ask: "What's hard about [problem you'd solve]? What have you tried? What would the perfect solution look like?" Use this to design your offer.

## Module 7: Taxes, Currency, Inflation

### Lesson 7.1 — Taxes 101

When you earn money, the government takes a portion. This is tax. In Nigeria:

- Personal income tax: 7%–24% depending on income level
- VAT (added to purchases): 7.5%
- Companies pay corporate tax separately

This sounds annoying but taxes pay for roads, schools, hospitals (theoretically). Every adult earner pays them.

**For teen side hustles in Nigeria:** Below a certain income threshold, you're effectively not taxable. As your business grows, you'll need to register (CAC) and start filing returns. Get a basic accountant when you cross ₦500,000/month consistently.

### Lesson 7.2 — Inflation: the silent thief

**Inflation** is when prices go up over time, which means your money buys less.

If a bag of rice cost ₦5,000 last year and ₦7,000 this year, that's 40% inflation on rice. Money you "saved" actually lost value if it just sat in a bank account earning 4%.

Nigerian inflation has been brutal recently — sometimes 25%+ annually. This is why:

1. Money sitting in regular savings accounts loses value
2. Investing matters more than ever
3. Some Nigerians put part of their savings in dollars
4. Real assets (land, businesses) often beat inflation

### Lesson 7.3 — Currency and exchange rates

When you see "USD/NGN: 1,500," that means 1 US dollar costs 1,500 naira. Six years ago it was around 360. The naira has lost massive value.

**Why this matters:**

- Things imported in dollars (electronics, fuel, medicine) get more expensive
- People with dollar income (some freelancers, exporters) get richer in naira terms
- Saving in dollars (legally, through licensed platforms) is one way Nigerians protect savings

For teens: when you start freelancing, working for international clients who pay in dollars or euros multiplies your earning power dramatically. A US client paying $20/hour pays you ₦30,000/hour. A Nigerian client at the same skill level might pay ₦3,000/hour.

### Lesson 7.4 — The official vs parallel market

Nigeria has multiple exchange rates: official rate (CBN), parallel market rate (the "real" street rate), and various others. They've recently converged but historically had huge gaps.

You don't need to understand all of this now. Just know: when sending or receiving money internationally, check the current rates on a real source (xe.com, official bank rates) so you don't get cheated.

### Exercise 7.1 — Inflation math

Find out: what did a loaf of bread cost in your city 5 years ago vs today? Calculate the percentage increase. Now imagine your savings grew at 5%/year. Did your purchasing power go up or down?

### Exercise 7.2 — Currency tracker

For one month, write down the USD/NGN rate every Monday. Notice if it moves up or down. Start understanding what's normal volatility vs major shifts.

### Exercise 7.3 — Earn in dollars (research)

Identify three platforms where teens or young adults can earn in dollars from Nigeria:
- Freelance platforms (Upwork, Fiverr)
- Content monetization (YouTube, TikTok creator fund where available)
- Selling digital products to global audience

Pick one to actually try in the next year.

## Module 8: Wealth Thinking and Your 5-Year Plan

### Lesson 8.1 — Wealth vs income

These aren't the same thing. **Income** is money coming in. **Wealth** is what you've accumulated.

A doctor earning ₦5 million/year who spends ₦5 million/year is not wealthy. A teacher earning ₦800,000/year who saves ₦200,000 of it for 30 years and invests well *will be* wealthy.

The road to wealth: spend less than you earn, invest the difference, repeat for decades.

### Lesson 8.2 — Multiple income streams

The wealthiest people don't rely on one source of money. Build streams over time:

1. **Active income** — your main job/business
2. **Side income** — second skill or hustle
3. **Investment income** — money making more money
4. **Passive income** — products, rents, royalties

You don't need all four at 16. But by 30, you should be working on three of them.

### Lesson 8.3 — The lifestyle inflation trap

Most people raise their spending every time their income rises. Get a raise, lease a bigger car. Earn more, eat at fancier restaurants. Your bank balance never changes.

The wealthy do the opposite. They keep their lifestyle modest, even when income grows. The gap between income and lifestyle is where wealth gets built.

This doesn't mean live like a monk. It means: when you start earning more, raise your savings rate before you raise your spending.

### Lesson 8.4 — The 5-year financial plan

By the time you're 25, where do you want to be financially? Reverse-engineer from there.

**Sample 5-year plan for a 15-year-old:**

- Year 1 (age 16): Open first savings account. Save first ₦50,000. Make first ₦10,000 from a side hustle.
- Year 2 (age 17): Build emergency fund (₦100,000). Start one consistent side income stream.
- Year 3 (age 18): Open investment account. Make first investment. Earn ₦500,000+/year from side work.
- Year 4 (age 19): Investment portfolio crosses ₦500,000. Diversify income to two streams.
- Year 5 (age 20): Net worth ₦2 million+. Three income sources. No bad debt.

These numbers are illustrative. Yours will look different. The point is having numbers and a timeline at all.

### Lesson 8.5 — Money rules to live by

These will compound in your life if you take them seriously:

1. **Pay yourself first.** Save before you spend, not what's left over.
2. **Time in the market beats timing the market.** Start investing early, even small amounts.
3. **Avoid lifestyle inflation.** Spend slower than your income grows.
4. **Don't lend money you can't afford to lose.** Lending to friends/family ends friendships.
5. **Track everything.** What gets measured improves.
6. **Marry someone with similar money values.** This isn't a money course problem, but it's a money problem.
7. **Never invest in what you don't understand.**
8. **Build skills before assets.** Skills earn you the money you'll invest.
9. **Buy assets, not liabilities.** An asset puts money in your pocket. A liability takes it out.
10. **Keep learning.** Money rules change. The principles don't.

### Capstone Project — Your Financial Foundation

Build this over 4 weeks:

**Week 1:** Track every transaction. Build your real budget based on data.

**Week 2:** Open a savings account if you don't have one. Start an emergency fund (target: 1 month of expenses).

**Week 3:** Pick one side hustle. Make your first sale.

**Week 4:** Open an investment account (with a guardian if needed). Research one investment. Plan your first contribution.

**Deliverable:** A 1-page document showing:
- Your monthly budget
- Your savings goal and current progress
- Your side hustle and first earnings
- Your investment plan for the next 12 months

Save this. Update it every 6 months for the next decade.
